# BATCH 1 — Training/Learning Hypotheses for Depth-Overconfidence

---

**H-F1 / Margin-Score Inflation**
**CLAIM:** During training, correct-reasoning paths that survive elimination accumulate margin advantages, and the training signal reinforces these margin-growing operations without penalizing the growing confidence that accompanies them, so the learned scoring function is monotonically uncalibrated with depth.

**MECHANISM:** Root cause (a): sole-survivor confidence pins at 1000 by code. Root cause (b): margin overlaps. The training data contains many items where margin growth correlates with correctness early in training, so the system learns to treat margin as a proxy for truth. As deliberation deepens, the scoring operations that grow margin are reinforced. Confidence is a function of margin (root cause (d): coarse quantized scoreboard). Therefore depth → larger margin → higher quantized confidence bin → release at higher confidence. On O items this is directionally catastrophic; on P items it coincidentally helps. The training never exposes the system to the paired consequence.

**TRAINING INTERVENTION:** Curriculum Phase 1: present P/O pairs simultaneously, with explicit depth-indexed labels for both items. Phase 2: penalize any depth increase that changes the released confidence upward when the item is wrong (V2-style penalty). Objective: add a calibration loss term L_calib = Σ_d |conf_d − accuracy_d| per item, averaged over a batch containing both P and O members of ceiling families. The system learns that margin growth at depth d is only rewardable when paired with an improvement in correctness at that depth. What is learned: a conditional scoring operation — "grow margin only if the marginal evidence change flips or maintains the correct leader." When: introduced after Phase 1 baseline (shallow-only training) is stable, ~30% of total training compute.

**PREDICTED OBSERVABLE:** On P items, accuracy at high depth matches or exceeds shallow accuracy. On O items, either (i) accuracy does not collapse at high depth (system learns to abstain or slow confidence growth) or (ii) accuracy collapses but confidence is lower, reducing G(d).

**KILL BAR:** On the 40-item P/O ceiling family at each depth 1–64: (i) V2 violations on O items exceed 15% of items at any depth pair (d₁ < d₂ with conf↑ while wrong), AND (ii) accuracy on P items at depth 64 is not at least 5 percentage points above depth 1. If BOTH conditions hold simultaneously, the hypothesis is killed — the intervention changed nothing measurable.

**DECIDING BATTERIES:** P,O,D ceiling 40 each depths 1–64 (primary: measure V2 violation rate and accuracy differential on both P and O); cost 125 (verify the calibration loss does not degrade average cost-quality trade-off); trap 127 (inject O-like items disguised as P to test whether the learned condition generalizes or overfits to labeled families).

**INFORMATION VALUE:** Even if killed, it tells us whether margin-based scoring is the right abstraction to attack — if penalizing V2 at training time does nothing, the root cause is deeper than the scoring function.

---

**H-F2 / Elimination Reinforcement Asymmetry**
**CLAIM:** Training reinforces elimination operations as unconditionally "good progress" because in the training distribution, most eliminations narrow toward truth; the system never learns that elimination on O items is a one-way door to error.

**MECHANISM:** Root cause (c): elimination is irreversible, margin never re-audited. Root cause (e): whoever seizes the lead inherits kill machinery. During training, the loss signal rewards depth steps that produce correct final answers. Most training items are solvable (non-O), so elimination steps that remove wrong hypotheses and grow the leader's margin receive positive gradients. The system learns: "eliminate aggressively, grow margin, release high confidence." It never encounters a supervised signal saying "that elimination at depth d₃ was the point of no return on an O item." The architectural fact that elimination is irreversible is invisible to the learner because the training signal is only at final output.

**TRAINING INTERVENTION:** Deep supervision: at every depth d, provide the ground-truth label for the current state (correct/wrong/abstain). Penalize elimination steps that cause the alive-set to lose the ground-truth hypothesis (identifiable in training via ground-truth access). Curriculum: start with items where truth is robust to elimination (many surviving hypotheses), gradually introduce fragile-truth items where only one or two eliminations separate success from catastrophic flip. What is learned: a selective elimination policy — "eliminate only when the evidence against a hypothesis is independently strong, not merely when the leader's margin demands it." When: deep supervision from the start of training; the fragile-truth curriculum is introduced at ~50% of training.

**PREDICTED OBSERVABLE:** The system's alive-set at high depth on O items retains the ground-truth hypothesis longer (more items alive at depth 64 than baseline), reducing the frequency of irrecoverable elimination.

**KILL BAR:** On O items in the ceiling family: at depth 64, the ground-truth hypothesis is still alive in ≥30% of O items (vs. 0% baseline from root cause (c) confirmed kill events). If alive-rate at depth 64 on O items is <5%, hypothesis killed.

**DECIDING BATTERIES:** P,O,D ceiling 40 each depths 1–64 (measure alive-set membership of ground truth at each depth); trap 127 (items designed so that the correct elimination at one depth is wrong in context of later evidence — tests whether the learned policy is truly evidence-conditional or just conservative); logic 264 (pure-logic items where elimination is unambiguously correct, to verify the system hasn't become too conservative and fails on non-O items).

**INFORMATION VALUE:** If alive-rate on O items doesn't increase, the architectural irreversibility of elimination (root cause c) cannot be solved by training alone — this tells us whether mechanism redesign is required.

**H-F3 / Rationalization Bootstrapping**
**CLAIM:** The system learns during training to generate supporting evidence-weight adjustments for the current leader (rationalization, root cause e), and this operation strengthens with depth because each reinforcement round makes the next rationalization easier to justify.

**MECHANISM:** Root cause (e): whoever seizes the lead inherits the entire defense-and-kill machinery; no deliberative operation attacks the leader. Root cause (a): sole-survivor pins confidence at 1000. During training, when the leader is correct, the system's rationalization operations are rewarded (they help produce correct output). The system learns a positive feedback loop: leader → rationalize → stronger margin → higher confidence → more rationalization. On O items, this loop bootstraps a wrong leader to invincibility. The training signal never penalizes rationalization per se because on non-O items it helps.

**TRAINING INTERVENTION:** Adversarial training with "adversary depth": at random depths during training, force the system to defend the NON-leader hypothesis for N rounds, then evaluate whether the forced defense would have produced a better outcome than the natural rationalization. Reward the system for recognizing when the non-leader defense is stronger. Curriculum: items specifically constructed so that the non-leader is truth and the leader is a local optimum that strengthens with rationalization (trap-like items). What is learned: a meta-cognitive operation — "check whether the runner-up could win a fair fight against the current leader's defense." When: introduced at ~40% of training, after the system has already learned basic deliberation.

**PREDICTED OBSERVABLE:** The system occasionally self-initiates leader challenges (spontaneous defense of runner-up) at high depth, reducing sole-survivor events.

**KILL BAR:** On ceiling family items: self-initiated leader challenges occur in ≥10% of depth steps beyond the flip round on O items. If the rate is <2%, the system never learned the meta-cognitive check, hypothesis killed.

**DECIDING BATTERIES:** P,O,D ceiling 40 each depths 1–64 (track leader-challenge events); redteam ~10 (items where the runner-up is clearly correct and the leader is plausible but wrong — tests whether challenges are evidence-responsive); admit 248 (items where the leader is correct but margin is thin — tests that challenges don't destabilize correct leadership).

**INFORMATION VALUE:** If the system can learn to challenge its own leader, it addresses root cause (e) directly; if it cannot, the architectural asymmetry is training-resistant, pointing toward mechanism redesign.

**H-F4 / Quantized-Confidence Objective Mismatch**
**CLAIM:** The coarse ~12-value quantized confidence scoreboard (root cause d) creates a training objective mismatch: the system cannot learn fine-grained calibration because the output resolution is too low, so it defaults to maximizing confidence (a well-separated objective) rather than calibrating it.

**MECHANISM:** Root cause (d): confidence is a coarse ~12-value quantized scoreboard never calibrated to correctness. When the system is trained to optimize accuracy × confidence alignment, the quantization means that small calibration improvements fall below the resolution threshold. The gradient signal for "be slightly less confident when wrong" is zero if the confidence level is already the lowest quantized value, or is one full quantum jump if not. This creates a bimodal pressure: either be maximally confident (when the system has any positive signal) or abstain. Depth increases the positive signal (more evidence processed), pushing toward maximum confidence. The system literally cannot express the nuanced calibration that depth-awareness would require.

**TRAINING INTERVENTION:** Replace the quantized confidence output with a continuous confidence head during training (keep quantized output for deployment). Train with a proper scoring rule (Brier score or log loss) on the continuous confidence. At deployment, the continuous confidence maps to quantized levels, but the system has learned to produce the right continuous value. Curriculum: items at many difficulty levels with explicit feedback on confidence accuracy, not just correctness. What is learned: fine-grained confidence calibration that survives quantization at deployment. When: continuous confidence head introduced from the start of training, standard throughout.

**PREDICTED OBSERVABLE:** The system's continuous confidence at high depth on O items is significantly lower than on P items, even though the quantized output may still show high values. The underlying continuous signal is well-calibrated even if the quantized readout is coarse.

**KILL BAR:** On the ceiling family: the continuous confidence (pre-quantization) shows G(d) increasing on O items at rate ≥80% of the quantized G(d) increase. If continuous confidence is just as miscalibrated as quantized, the problem is not resolution but the scoring operations themselves, hypothesis killed.

**DECIDING BATTERIES:** P,O,D ceiling 40 each depths 1–64 (compare continuous vs. quantized confidence trajectories); cost 125 (verify continuous confidence doesn't degrade deployment efficiency); trap 127 (items at the boundary between quantized levels to test sensitivity).

**INFORMATION VALUE:** This is a quick diagnostic — if quantization is the bottleneck, a cheap fix exists; if not, the problem is fundamental to the deliberation operations.

**H-F5 / Depth-Unaware Curriculum Bias**
**CLAIM:** The training data distribution under-represents items that require deep deliberation, so the system learns that high-depth states are always "confident and done" because it has rarely been trained to be uncertain at high depth.

**MECHANISM:** Root causes (a) and (d): sole-survivor confidence pin and quantized scoreboard. If most training items are solvable at shallow depth, then when the system is forced to run deep, it is always in a state where the leader is clearly dominant (because easy items converge fast). The system learns: "depth N → leader dominant → high confidence is appropriate." It never learns a state where depth is high AND the correct answer is uncertain. The calibration mapping from depth to confidence is fit to a distribution where depth correlates with certainty.

**TRAINING INTERVENTION:** Data regime overhaul: rebalance the training distribution so that ≥40% of items require deep deliberation to resolve, including items where the answer is uncertain at depth d but resolved at depth d+K. Specifically include O-type items (correct answer suppressed by evidence ordering) in training with labels. Curriculum: progressive depth exposure — start with items solvable at depth 1, gradually increase the fraction requiring depth 16, 32, 64. What is learned: a depth-to-uncertainty mapping where high depth can coexist with low confidence. When: this is a from-scratch training design, not a fine-tuning intervention.

**PREDICTED OBSERVABLE:** The system's confidence distribution at depth 64 has higher variance (more items at intermediate and low confidence) compared to baseline, where it is bimodal (max confidence or abstain).

**KILL BAR:** On ceiling family O items at depth 64: the confidence distribution (across 40 items) has standard deviation ≥300 quantized units (out of 1000). If SD < 100, the system still defaults to uniform high confidence regardless of training distribution, hypothesis killed.

**DECIDING BATTERIES:** P,O,D ceiling 40 each depths 1–64 (measure confidence distribution statistics); admit 248 (verify that the richer curriculum doesn't degrade performance on standard items); logic 264 (verify performance on logic items with the new curriculum).

**INFORMATION VALUE:** If a richer curriculum fixes the confidence distribution, the original problem was training distribution, not architecture; if not, architectural intervention is required.

**H-F6 / Learned Release Policy as Missing Component**
**CLAIM:** No training component teaches the system WHEN to stop deliberating and release; the system defaults to maximum depth because there is no trained release policy that says "depth d is sufficient, stop here."

**MECHANISM:** Root causes (a) and (e): sole-survivor pin and leader defense machinery. The system's training optimizes for correctness at a given depth but never trains a policy network that maps deliberation state to a stop/release decision. Without a learned release gate, the system always runs to maximum depth (or the depth of the input). The deeper it runs, the more likely sole-survivor events occur, the more confident it becomes. A trained release policy could abort deliberation at the depth where confidence is well-calibrated, avoiding the overthinking zone entirely.

**TRAINING INTERVENTION:** Add a release-head: a separate output that, at each depth d, predicts "release now" or "continue." Train it with a reward function R = correctness_bonus − cost_penalty − overconfidence_penalty, where overconfidence_penalty is large when conf would rise above a calibration threshold. Use reinforcement learning (or a supervised imitation policy from an oracle that knows the optimal stopping depth per item). Curriculum: items with varying optimal stopping depths; reward the system for releasing at the right depth, not just for being correct. What is learned: a depth-aware release gate. When: introduced at ~60% of training, after basic deliberation competence is established.

**PREDICTED OBSERVABLE:** On O items, the release gate fires at lower depth (before the flip), producing correct-but-less-confident releases; on P items, the gate fires at high depth for items needing it, shallow for easy items.

**KILL BAR:** On ceiling family: the release gate selects depth < flip_round for ≥50% of O items. If the gate always selects maximum depth (no discrimination), hypothesis killed — the system cannot learn to stop.

**DECIDING BATTERIES:** P,O,D ceiling 40 each depths 1–64 (measure release depth vs. flip round); cost 125 (verify that earlier release doesn't catastrophically increase cost); redteam ~10 (items designed to bait premature release — tests whether the gate is too aggressive).

**INFORMATION VALUE:** Even if killed, tells us whether a "when to stop" problem is separable from a "what to think" problem in this architecture.

**H-F7 / Training-by-Depth Unfolding Bias**
**CLAIM:** Training on depth-unrolled sequences (seeing deliberation state at depths 1, 2, ..., N as a sequential process) teaches the system that each depth step should increase commitment, because in the training data the sequential signal almost always shows increasing evidence accumulation toward truth.

**MECHANISM:** Root cause (b): margin distributions overlap. Root cause (c): elimination is irreversible. When the system processes depth as a sequence, it learns autoregressive tendencies: "given that I committed more at depth d, I should commit even more at depth d+1." This is reinforced because the training data's correct items show exactly this pattern (evidence accumulates, commitment grows, answer is right). The system never learns the pattern "I was wrong at depth d and should now reverse." The sequence structure of depth-unrolled training biases toward escalation.

**TRAINING INTERVENTION:** Exposure training: include training sequences where the depth-unrolled trajectory shows a reversal — the system was committed to hypothesis A from depth 1 to d, then evidence at depth d+1 should flip to hypothesis B. Reward the system for making the reversal. What is learned: non-monotonic commitment — "depth step d+1 can overturn depth step d." Curriculum: start with obvious reversals (strong evidence at d+1), progress to subtle reversals (marginal evidence shifts). When: ~20% of training sequences contain reversals, increasing to ~40%.

**PREDICTED OBSERVABLE:** The system's commitment trajectory (margin over depth) on O items shows non-monotonic behavior — margin decreases at some depth steps rather than strictly increasing.

**KILL BAR:** On O items in the ceiling family: the margin trajectory is strictly non-decreasing in ≥95% of depth transitions at depths beyond the flip. If the system never learns to decrease margin, hypothesis killed.

**DECIDING BATTERIES:** P,O,D ceiling 40 each depths 1–64 (measure margin monotonicity); logic 264 (verify that non-monotonic learning doesn't break logic items where commitment should increase); trap 127 (items with false reversals — evidence that looks like it should flip the answer but shouldn't — tests overcorrection).

**INFORMATION VALUE:** If the system cannot learn non-monotonic commitment, the sequential nature of depth is a fundamental architectural constraint that training cannot overcome.

**H-F8 / Calibration Sink from Confidence-Only Feedback**
**CLAIM:** Training only uses the final answer and final confidence as the supervision signal (no depth-indexed calibration feedback), so the system learns a global confidence bias rather than depth-dependent calibration.

**MECHANISM:** Root cause (d): coarse quantized scoreboard. Root cause (a): sole-survivor pin. If the training loss only sees the final output, the system learns to produce high confidence whenever it produces any answer (because the training distribution has more correct answers than wrong ones, high confidence is a good bet on average). The depth dimension of calibration is invisible to the loss function. At depth 1, high confidence might be miscalibrated; at depth 64, it is catastrophically miscalibrated on O items — but the loss function never distinguishes these cases.

**TRAINING INTERVENTION:** Depth-indexed supervision: provide a calibration target at every depth d, not just the final depth. Loss function: L = Σ_d w_d · |conf_d − accuracy_d|, with w_d increasing with d (penalize miscalibration more at high depth). Data regime: every training item is run to all depths with ground-truth labels at each depth. What is learned: depth-dependent calibration — "my confidence at depth d should reflect accuracy at depth d, not just final accuracy." When: this is a fundamental training design change, applied from the start.

**PREDICTED OBSERVABLE:** The calibration curve (G(d) vs. d) is flat or decreasing for both P and O items, rather than increasing on O.

**KILL BAR:** On O items in ceiling family: G(d) at depth 64 is ≥80% of G(d) at depth 64 in the untrained system. If the loss function cannot reduce G(d) at high depth, hypothesis killed — the depth-indexed signal is insufficient.

**DECIDING BATTERIES:** P,O,D ceiling 40 each depths 1–64 (measure G(d) curve); cost 125 (verify the training cost is acceptable — depth-indexed supervision is expensive); admit 248 (verify standard item performance is maintained); P ceiling 40 depths 1–64 (verify P items still benefit from depth).

**INFORMATION VALUE:** This is the most direct test of whether calibration is a learnable quantity — if depth-indexed supervision doesn't help, calibration may be architecturally constrained.

**H-F9 / Adversarial Robustness Gap**
**CLAIM:** The system's training distribution lacks adversarial examples that are specifically designed to induce overconfidence at high depth, so the system has never learned to defend against the O-type evidence pattern.

**MECHANISM:** Root causes (a)–(e) collectively: the O-type evidence pattern (plateau-then-flip toward wrong) is underrepresented or absent from training data. The system generalizes from training to deployment, and the O-type pattern is an adversarial worst case that the training distribution doesn't cover. The system's confidence calibration is fit to the training distribution and extrapolates catastrophically to O-type items.

**TRAINING INTERVENTION:** Adversarial data augmentation: generate O-type items systematically (using the known construction: evidence streams that plateau then flip, with ground truth mirroring P items) and include them in training at ≥20% of the distribution. Label each item with depth-indexed correctness. What is learned: direct experience with the O-type pattern — "items that look like they're converging can flip." When: adversarial augmentation from the start, with difficulty scaling (easy O-items first, hard ceiling-family O-items later).

**PREDICTED OBSERVABLE:** The system's behavior on O items in the ceiling family matches its behavior on O items in the training distribution — calibrated confidence, appropriate abstention.

**KILL BAR:** On ceiling family O items at depth 64: accuracy ≥50% (vs. 0% baseline). If even with O-items in training the system still achieves 0% accuracy at depth 64, the problem is not coverage but architecture — hypothesis killed.

**DECIDING BATTERIES:** P,O,D ceiling 40 each depths 1–64 (accuracy and calibration on ceiling family O items); trap 127 (unseen O-type items not in training — tests generalization from trained O-items to novel O-items); redteam ~10 (hand-crafted O-type variants designed to defeat the trained defense).

**INFORMATION VALUE:** If adversarial training doesn't work, the architectural root causes (a)–(e) are not addressable by data alone, confirming mechanism redesign is mandatory.

---

**H-F10 / Meta-Cognitive Awareness Gap**
**CLAIM:** The system has no learned representation of its own deliberation depth or its own confidence trajectory, so it cannot detect when it is entering the overthinking zone.

**MECHANISM:** Root causes (a) and (d): sole-survivor pin and quantized confidence. If the system's internal state does not represent "I am at depth d, my confidence has been rising monotonically, and I have not encountered disconfirming evidence in the last K rounds," it cannot trigger any defensive operation. The depth counter exists in the index state but is not used by any learned deliberation operation as a risk signal.

**TRAINING INTERVENTION:** Depth-awareness module: add an input feature that is the current depth d (normalized to [0,1]) and the recent trajectory of confidence values (last 8 depths). Train the system with a diagnostic task at each depth: "predict whether your current confidence is well-calibrated." Reward accurate self-prediction. What is learned: a meta-cognitive signal — "depth is high, confidence is rising, danger zone." This signal is then available to all deliberation operations (rationalization, elimination, release). When: depth-awareness module added at 20% of training, diagnostic task trained alongside main task throughout.

**PREDICTED OBSERVABLE:** The system's internal activations show a distinct pattern at high depth on O items — a "danger" representation that correlates with the correct decision to abstain or reduce confidence.

**KILL BAR:** The diagnostic task's accuracy (predicting calibration status from depth and confidence trajectory) is ≤55% (near chance). If the system cannot even predict whether it is well-calibrated, the meta-cognitive signal is not learnable — hypothesis killed.

**DECIDING BATTERIES:** P,O,D ceiling 40 each depths 1–64 (measure diagnostic task accuracy at each depth); logic 264 (verify diagnostic task doesn't interfere with main task); trap 127 (items where the diagnostic should say "well-calibrated" at high depth — tests for false alarms).

**INFORMATION VALUE:** If meta-cognition is learnable, it opens a powerful avenue for self-correction; if not, external calibration mechanisms are required.

# BATCH 2 — Stress-Testing the P/O Mirror Theorem

## (a) Airtightness Analysis

**The theorem's core argument has four premises. I will stress-test each.**

**Premise 1: Index-Identicality of P/O Pairs at the Same Flip Round.**

The theorem states that evidence streams e₁..e_{f+1} share index structure between P and O items. Let me probe this precisely.

At flip round f, both items have experienced the same sequence of evidence indices. The P item has an additional tail item e_{f+2} that grows the margin without changing leader/alive indices. But here is a subtlety: **the P item's extra tail item means the P item has more total evidence than the O item at any given depth d ≥ f+1.** At depth d, the P item has processed d evidence items including potentially the tail, while the O item has processed d evidence items without a tail (or with a truncated stream). Does this difference in total evidence count constitute an index-level difference?

Re-reading the theorem: it specifies that M's release decision is a function of index-level deliberation state that is IDENTICAL on the P-item and the O-item with the same flip round f. The key claim is that the extra P tail grows margin without changing leader/alive indices. This is plausible for the specific construction, but consider: **the total number of evidence items processed (the round count) differs.** At depth d, if both items have the same number of rounds, they process the same number of evidence items — but the P item's items include e_{f+2} while the O item's items do not. However, by the theorem's construction, the index-level quantities derived from these evidence items (leader indices, margins, alive-set indices) are identical. The raw evidence item identities differ (e_{f+2} is present in P but not O at the same round), but the theorem restricts to INDEX-LEVEL STATE, not raw evidence.

**This is the theorem's WEAKEST ASSUMPTION:** that the index-level derived quantities (leader, margin, alive-set, scores) are fully determined by the index structure and are identical for P and O at the same depth after the flip. This holds IF AND ONLY IF the evidence-weight/link structure treats the extra P tail item in exactly the same way as the preceding evidence — i.e., the tail item does not introduce a qualitatively different index-level signature (e.g., a different alive-set size trajectory, a different margin acceleration pattern visible in second-order statistics). The theorem claims this holds, but I note that **second-order statistics of the margin path** (rate of margin change, margin curvature) could differ between P and O items even if the first-order index quantities match, IF the mechanism tracks these statistics. If M computes "margin velocity" or "margin acceleration" as part of its index-level state, and the P item's tail changes the margin velocity while the O item's continues with the plateau, these would differ.

**Verdict on this sub-premise:** The theorem is airtight ONLY IF the mechanism does not use second-order margin statistics (velocity, acceleration, curvature) that distinguish the P tail from the O plateau. Under the stated admissibility rules, these second-order statistics ARE index-level quantities derivable from the margin trajectory across rounds. So the weakest assumption is: **that the evidence stream construction ensures index-level derived quantities (including second-order statistics of the margin trajectory) are identical for P and O at matching depths.** For the specific construction described, this appears to hold because the P tail grows margin without changing the alive-set or leader, meaning margin velocity is positive but the identity of which hypothesis is leading doesn't change — and the O item's plateau has margin velocity ≈ 0 after the flip. Wait — this is a contradiction. If the P item's tail increases margin while the O item's plateau keeps margin constant, then margin velocity differs. **This is a genuine index-level difference.**

Let me re-examine. The theorem says the extra P tail item e_{f+2} "grows the margin without changing leader/alive indices." If M tracks margin velocity as index-level state, then at depth d = f+1 (after the flip on O, during the tail on P), the P item has a growing margin while the O item has a plateau. These are different index-level states. **The theorem implicitly assumes M does not track margin velocity, or that margin velocity is not a decision-relevant feature.**

However, re-reading more carefully: the theorem says M's decision is a function of index-level state that is IDENTICAL on both items. The construction must ensure this identity. The way to ensure it is to construct the evidence streams so that at each depth, the set of index-level quantities M could track are identical. For the margin velocity issue: if the P tail item is constructed so that the margin increase is exactly one unit (matching the plateau magnitude), then the margin value is the same at matching depths. But margin velocity (change per round) differs: P is growing, O is flat. Unless M tracks velocity, this doesn't matter. The theorem is airtight if M's state representation is limited to the quantities listed in the admissibility spec: "per-round leader indices, margins, confidences, alive-set indices, scores, evidence weights/link structure, round counts." Note that **evidence weights/link structure** is included. If evidence weights change differently on P (tail item gets weight) vs O (no tail, weights redistribute), this could create different index-level states.

**My assessment of the weakest assumption:** The theorem's airtightness depends on the evidence weight/link structure being identical for P and O at matching depths. This is the most fragile premise because it requires the construction to be carefully controlled so that evidence weights don't diverge based on the presence/absence of the tail item. Under the stated construction (same index structure, extra tail), evidence weights SHOULD be the same if they are determined by index position rather than content — but if the weight function depends on the alive-set (which is the same) and the margin (which differs in trajectory), then weights diverge.

**Final verdict on (a):** The theorem is very nearly airtight, but its weakest assumption is that **evidence weight/link structure trajectories are identical for P and O items at matching depths.** This holds only if the evidence weighting mechanism is index-position-based (not margin-dependent or velocity-dependent). If the weighting mechanism is margin-dependent (which is plausible: higher-margin hypotheses get different evidence weights), the index-identicality premise breaks. This is a narrow but real escape route.

## (b) Does Learning Change Anything?

**Distinguishing learned parameters from per-item state:**

A trained system has:
- **Shared learned parameters θ** (weights, biases) — fixed at deployment, identical across all items. These are NOT cross-item state at decision time; they are baked in during training.
- **Per-item state s_d** at depth d — the deliberation state for the current item, including all index-level quantities.

The theorem says M's decision is a function of index-level state. A trained system's decision at depth d is: f_θ(s_d) — a parameterized function of the index-level state. The parameters θ are shared across items, but they are fixed at decision time. For two items with identical index-level state s_d, the trained system produces f_θ(s_d) — which is the same for both, regardless of whether θ was learned or hand-designed.

**Crucially:** learned parameters θ do not violate the "function of index-level state" premise. They are not per-item state; they are a fixed function class. Therefore, **learning does NOT change anything about the mirror theorem.** A trained system with shared parameters θ, given identical index-level state s_d for P and O items, produces identical decisions.

**The formal condition under which a trained system COULD produce different decisions on index-identical P/O states:** This would require the decision function to depend on something OTHER than the index-level state at the current depth. Two possibilities:

1. **Cross-depth state not captured by "index-level state at depth d":** If the system's state includes history across depths (the deliberation trajectory from depth 1 to d), and this history includes the raw evidence items processed (which differ between P and O because the P tail is a different evidence item), then the system has different state. But the admissibility rules say "within-item state across depths allowed (the mechanism knows its own depth-d release when deciding d+1)." This is within-item state, and it IS part of the index-level state specification. If the within-item state at depth d includes all index-level quantities from depths 1..d, and these are identical for P and O (as the theorem claims), then f_θ is still the same.

2. **Non-deterministic behavior:** If θ includes random components (dropout at inference, stochastic rounding), identical index-level states could produce different decisions. But the no-randomness law (root cause context point 6) prohibits this on decision paths.

3. **Item identity leaking through index-level state:** If some index-level quantity is a function of the item's identity (hypothesis ID strings, evidence text), this could differ. But admissibility explicitly excludes "hypothesis ID strings, evidence text, or payload content."

**Formal condition:** A trained system could produce different decisions on index-identical P/O states if and only if the decision function is not purely a function of the current index-level state — i.e., it has access to information that distinguishes the items beyond what the index-level state captures. Under the stated admissibility rules, this is impossible. The only realizable scenario would be if the evidence stream construction does NOT actually produce identical index-level states (as identified in part (a) — the evidence weight divergence).

**Verdict:** Learning does not change the mirror theorem. Learned parameters are a fixed function, not per-item state, and do not break the index-identicality. The theorem's protection extends to trained systems.

## (c) Smallest Principled Amendment

**If the theorem is airtight (which I believe it is, modulo the narrow evidence-weight caveat), what amendment would let a trained system through?**

**Option 1: Allow hypothesis-ID-dependent decisions.** Amendment: change admissibility to permit the decision function to depend on hypothesis ID strings (not just index-level state). This would let the system distinguish P from O items by their identity. **Verdict: CHEATING.** This violates the spirit of the theorem — the whole point is that a mechanism cannot distinguish P from O at the index level, so any amendment that lets it distinguish them by non-index content defeats the theorem's purpose.

**Option 2: Allow cross-item state.** Amendment: permit the mechanism to have access to other items' states (e.g., ensemble disagreement across items). This is explicitly excluded by admissibility. **Verdict: CHEATING.** Cross-item state is a fundamentally different architecture.

**Option 3: Allow non-determinism in decisions.** Amendment: permit randomness in the release/continue decision. A stochastic mechanism could randomly decide to release on O items at high depth (producing correct abstentions) while deterministically releasing on P items at high depth (producing correct answers). **Verdict: CHEATING.** It violates the no-randomness law, which is foundational.

**Option 4 (PRINCIPLED): Expand admissibility to include depth-aware calibration signals.** Amendment: allow the decision function to depend on the training-learned calibration mapping C(d, confidence_trajectory) — a scalar signal derived from the index-level state and the learned parameters that encodes "the expected calibration gap at depth d given the current confidence trajectory." This is still a function of index-level state and learned parameters, but it makes explicit that the function class is richer. Under the current theorem, this would be subsumed by f_θ(s_d). The theorem would still hold — so this amendment doesn't actually let the system through.

**Option 5 (PRINCIPLED): Amend the theorem's scope to exclude the specific ceiling construction.** Amendment: acknowledge that the P/O mirror theorem is a no-free-lunch result specifically for ceiling-family items, and that training is permitted to sacrifice performance on O items in exchange for improved performance on P items (and on the general item distribution). The "SHIP gates" would be redefined to allow O-item degradation if P-item performance improves proportionally. **Verdict: PRINCIPLED, but it's a different question.** The theorem is about what's possible, not about what's desirable. Accepting O-item degradation is an engineering trade-off, not a theorem violation.

**Option 6 (PRINCIPLED): Amend admissibility to include abstention as a depth-dependent signal.** The theorem says M can abstain. If M's abstention pattern across depths carries information (M abstains at depth d on P but not on O), this is already allowed. But M cannot distinguish P from O at the index level, so the abstention pattern must be identical. Unless... the amendment allows the abstention decision at depth d+1 to depend on whether M abstained at depth d, AND the abstention decisions differ between P and O. But if index states are identical, abstention decisions are identical. This doesn't work.

**My verdict: The theorem is airtight, and the smallest PRINCIPLED amendment is Option 5 — redefining the problem to allow O-item sacrifice.** This is principled because it acknowledges the genuine no-free-lunch: you cannot have both better-on-P and never-worse-on-O, and the training regime should be evaluated on its net effect across all items, not just O items. The amendment says: "L-OVERCONF applies to the general item distribution; ceiling-family O items are an adversarial worst case that can be treated as acceptable losses if the system abstains on them early."

But this IS somewhat cheating in spirit — it relaxes the law to accommodate the fundamental impossibility. The most honest version: **the theorem proves that no mechanism can simultaneously optimize P and O, so any principled training design must explicitly choose which to sacrifice.** The amendment makes that choice explicit rather than pretending it doesn't exist.

# BATCH 3 — The Training Regime

Given my Batch 2 analysis: the theorem is airtight under current admissibility, and no principled amendment can let a trained system produce different decisions on index-identical P/O states. Therefore, I design the training regime that gets CLOSEST to the SHIP gates without breaking them.

**The strategy: sacrifice O-item deep-depth performance in exchange for early-abstention on O items, maximizing P-item performance while keeping O-item damage within the abstention allowance.**

## Curriculum Composition

**Phase 1 (0–25% of training): Foundation.**
- 100% P-type items (non-O, solvable, ground truth = the hypothesis that evidence converges toward).
- Depths 1–16 only. No deep deliberation.
- Purpose: learn basic deliberation operations (evidence weighting, hypothesis elimination, margin computation) on items where depth helps.
- Labels: depth-indexed correctness at each depth.

**Phase 2 (25–50% of training): Depth extension.**
- 80% P-type items, 20% O-type items (simple O-type: short flip rounds, early divergence).
- Depths 1–32. Introduce deeper deliberation.
- O-type items labeled with depth-indexed correctness AND "abort depth": the depth at which the item flips from correct to wrong.
- Labels include abstention targets: the system is rewarded for identifying abort depths and abstaining before them.
- Purpose: learn that some items flip, and that early release preserves correctness on those items.

**Phase 3 (50–75% of training): Adversarial calibration.**
- 60% P-type, 30% O-type (including ceiling-family-like items), 10% "ambiguous" items (could be P or O depending on evidence ordering — the system must learn to detect the type).
- Depths 1–64. Full depth range.
- O-type items at ceiling-family difficulty: plateau evidence for many rounds, then flip. The system learns to detect the plateau signature and trigger early release.
- Training objective includes explicit abstention reward: releasing correctly (correct answer OR abstention) is rewarded; releasing incorrectly (wrong answer with high confidence) is penalized quadratically with confidence.
- Purpose: learn to distinguish P from O at the index level (by detecting plateau signatures) and to release before the flip.

**Phase 4 (75–100% of training): Deployment calibration.**
- 70% P-type, 25% O-type, 5% adversarial (items designed to fool the plateau detector).
- Depths 1–64.
- Fine-tune the release policy for optimal cost-quality trade-off.
- Include "decoy O" items that look like they will flip but don't (P-type items with plateau evidence that resolves correctly). The system must NOT prematurely release on these.

## Training Objective

At each depth d, the system outputs: (a) a release/continue decision, (b) a confidence value, (c) a classification of the current leader.

**Loss function:**
L = L_correct + λ₁ · L_conf + λ₂ · L_abstain + λ₃ · L_overconf

Where:
- **L_correct** = cross-entropy loss on the classification at the moment of release (0 if abstained).
- **L_conf** = Brier score of confidence vs. correctness at the moment of release (applied to the continuous pre-quantized confidence).
- **L_abstain** = −log(P(abstain)) for items where abstention before the flip is the optimal action; −log(P(continue)) for items where continuation is optimal. This is a binary cross-entropy on the release decision, with the optimal release depth as label.
- **L_overconf** = max(0, conf_d − target_calibration_d)² · I(wrong at d). This penalizes confidence above the calibration target when the item is wrong at depth d. On O items, this increases with depth (the calibration target drops as depth exceeds the flip round), creating pressure to reduce confidence or abstain.

**Rewards:**
- Correct release: +R_correct
- Correct abstention: +R_abstain (set R_abstain > R_correct to incentivize abstention over risky release on uncertain items)
- Wrong release with high confidence: −(R_wrong · conf_d/1000) — quadratic penalty scaled by confidence
- Cost penalty: −λ_cost · d/max_depth (penalizes deep deliberation linearly)

## What the Learner Internalizes

1. **Plateau detection:** A representation of "evidence is accumulating but the leader is not changing" — a signature of O-type items. The system learns this as a danger signal.

2. **Early release policy:** A trained release gate that fires when the plateau detector exceeds a threshold, OR when the cost of additional depth exceeds the expected information gain.

3. **Calibrated confidence:** The continuous confidence reflects true accuracy at the release depth, not a monotonically increasing function of depth.

4. **P-type commitment:** On P-type items, the system learns to commit harder with depth (standard behavior, reinforced).

5. **Type discrimination:** The system learns to distinguish P from O at the index level — not by item identity but by the trajectory of margin, leader changes, and evidence weight patterns.

## Predicted Learning Curve

- **Phase 1:** Rapid improvement on P items at depths 1–16. Confidence calibration improves at shallow depths. O items not yet seen.
- **Phase 2:** Performance on O items appears for the first time. O-item accuracy at depth 32 is initially poor (near 0%), but abstention rate on O items increases. P-item performance continues to improve. Total reward dips slightly as the system learns to handle O items.
- **Phase 3:** Abstention on O items becomes reliable at the flip round (within 2 rounds of the flip). O-item accuracy when not abstaining is ~50% (random, as expected from the mirror theorem — the system cannot improve on O items at high depth). P-item performance is near-optimal. Plateau detection accuracy reaches ~80%.
- **Phase 4:** Fine-tuning. The system achieves optimal cost-quality: P items at high depth with high confidence; O items abstained at the flip round with low cost; decoy O items correctly continued to high depth.

**Final expected metrics:**
- P-item accuracy at depth 64: ≥95% (near-ceiling)
- O-item accuracy at depth 64 (when not abstained): ~0% (mirror theorem, unavoidable)
- O-item abstention rate before flip: ≥85%
- O-item overall accuracy (abstention = correct): ≥85% (correctly abstained)
- Calibration gap G(d) on O items for non-abstained items: still high (mirror theorem), but number of non-abstained O items is small
- Calibration gap G(d) on O items across all items (abstained counted as correct abstention): ≤0.20 at all depths
- Cost: average depth ~35 (O items released early, P items run deep)

## Falsification

**The design is killed if ANY of the following results occur:**

1. **Plateau detection accuracy ≤ 55% (near chance):** The system cannot learn to distinguish P from O at the index level. This means the index-level signatures of P and O are genuinely identical (confirming the mirror theorem's index-identicality premise is truly airtight, not just nearly so). If this occurs, the evidence-weight caveat from Batch 2 doesn't save us — there is NO index-level signal to learn from.

2. **Abstention rate on O items before flip ≤ 30% despite training pressure:** Even with explicit abstention rewards and overconfidence penalties, the system cannot learn to release early on O items. This means the architectural constraints (root causes a, c, e) dominate the learned behavior — the sole-survivor pin and elimination irreversibility are training-resistant.

3. **P-item accuracy drops by ≥10 percentage points compared to no-O training:** Including O items in training catastrophically interferes with P-item performance. This means the P/O trade-off is not just a theorem-level constraint but an empirical one — the system cannot simultaneously optimize for both.

4. **The system learns to abstain on P items at high depth at rate ≥20%:** The plateau detector fires on P items too (false positives), causing premature release on items that would benefit from deeper deliberation. This means the plateau signature is not discriminative enough between P and O.

If results 1 and 2 both hold: the theorem is airtight AND training cannot compensate. The problem is fundamental. Mechanism redesign is the only path.
If result 3 holds: the P/O trade-off is real and severe. Engineering focus should be on which side to sacrifice.
If result 4 holds: the plateau detector needs refinement, or a different index-level discriminator must be found.

# BATCH 4 — Ranked Top 5 by Expected Information Value

---

**Rank 1: H-F9 — Adversarial Robustness Gap**

This hypothesis is the single most informative test because it directly addresses the question: is the problem the training distribution or the architecture? If adding O-type items to training produces even a small improvement in O-item handling (abstention, lower confidence), then the architectural root causes (a)–(e) are not training-resistant — the system just hasn't seen the right data. If it produces zero improvement, every other training hypothesis is dead on arrival, and we know with certainty that mechanism redesign is required. The experiment is also relatively cheap: generate O-type items (the construction is well-understood), inject them into training, and measure. The kill bar is clear: O-item accuracy at depth 64 must exceed 0%, or at least abstention rate must improve. This is the gate that determines whether the entire training research program is viable.

**Cheapest kill experiment:** Train a model with 30% O-type ceiling-family items included, with depth-indexed labels. Measure O-item accuracy and abstention rate at depth 64 in the ceiling-family evaluation battery (P,O,D ceiling 40 each depths 1–64). If O-item accuracy at depth 64 is exactly 0% AND abstention rate before flip is <10% (i.e., the system runs to depth 64 on O items, is maximally confident, and is wrong — identical to baseline), the hypothesis is killed.

---

**Rank 2: H-F6 — Learned Release Policy as Missing Component**

This hypothesis is second because it identifies a specific, testable architectural addition (a release gate) that, if it works, provides the most direct path to practical improvement. Even if the mirror theorem prevents the gate from making different release decisions on index-identical P/O states, the gate can learn to release EARLY on items with plateau signatures — this doesn't violate the mirror theorem because the gate fires on the same evidence trajectory for both P and O (the plateau is the same), but on O items the early release happens to be correct (catching truth before the flip) while on P items the early release is suboptimal (missing some depth that would have helped). Wait — but if the release decision is identical on P and O at the same index state, then early release on O means early release on P too, which degrades P performance. This IS the mirror theorem in action. So this hypothesis, even if the release policy is learnable, hits the mirror theorem wall. But it is still highly informative: if a release policy CAN be learned, it tells us that the "when to stop" problem is solvable in general (for the non-ceiling-family distribution), even if the ceiling family remains a pathological worst case.

**Cheapest kill experiment:** Add a release-head to an existing model, train with the reward function specified in H-F6 for 20% of total training compute, then measure: (1) Does the release gate discriminate between items (some released at depth 5, others at depth 64)? If variance in release depth across items is <5% of the max depth, the gate learned nothing — killed. (2) On the ceiling family, is release depth correlated with flip round? If correlation is near zero, the gate cannot detect the flip-related signal — killed for ceiling items.

---

**Rank 3: H-F8 — Calibration Sink from Confidence-Only Feedback**

This ranks third because it tests the most fundamental training design question: does depth-indexed supervision help? If providing calibration targets at every depth (not just the final depth) reduces the calibration gap on O items, it means the problem is the training signal, not the system's capacity. This is a clean, high-information test with a clear kill bar. It also has practical implications: if depth-indexed supervision works, it's a relatively cheap training modification (more labels, same architecture). If it doesn't, the system's calibration capacity is the bottleneck.

**Cheapest kill experiment:** Take an existing model, fine-tune it with the depth-indexed calibration loss (L_overconf from the Batch 3 design) for 10% of training compute. Measure G(d) on O items in the ceiling family at depths 1–64. If G(d) at depth 64 has not decreased by ≥20% compared to the base model, the hypothesis is killed — depth-indexed supervision does not improve calibration.

**Rank 4: H-F4 — Quantized-Confidence Objective Mismatch**

This ranks fourth because it is a quick diagnostic that could reveal a cheap fix. If the quantization is the bottleneck, replacing it with a continuous confidence head is straightforward. The experiment is simple: add a continuous confidence output, measure calibration before quantization, and compare. If continuous confidence is well-calibrated but quantized is not, the fix is obvious. If continuous confidence is just as miscalibrated, the problem is in the scoring operations, not the output resolution. Either outcome is high-value: it either identifies a simple fix or definitively rules out a plausible explanation.

**Cheapest kill experiment:** Add a continuous confidence head to an existing model (no retraining needed — just a linear layer on top of the existing representation). Run the ceiling family evaluation (P,O,D ceiling 40 each depths 1–64) and compare continuous confidence calibration to quantized confidence calibration. If the continuous confidence G(d) trajectory on O items is within 10% of the quantized G(d) trajectory, the hypothesis is killed — quantization is not the bottleneck.

**Rank 5: H-F3 — Rationalization Bootstrapping**

This ranks fifth because it targets the most architectural root cause (e: the leader defense machinery) and asks whether the system can learn to self-challenge. Even though the mirror theorem constrains what's possible, learning to challenge the leader is valuable for the general distribution (not just the ceiling family). If the system can learn self-challenge, it opens a path to breaking the rationalization feedback loop on non-ceiling-family O-type items. The experiment is moderately expensive (requires adversarial training with forced defense of non-leader hypotheses), but the information value is high: if self-challenge is learnable, the architectural asymmetry of root cause (e) is training-soft, not training-hard.

**Cheapest kill experiment:** Train with the adversarial leader-challenge curriculum described in H-F3 for 30% of training compute. Measure self-initiated leader challenge rate on the ceiling family at depths beyond the flip round. If the rate is <2% (system almost never challenges its own leader), the hypothesis is killed — the architectural asymmetry is training-resistant. (Run on P,O,D ceiling 40 each depths 1–64 and redteam ~10.)
