S|The speed of light is the speed of electromagnetic waves.
S|Light travels at slower speed inside materials like glass or water; its highest speed is in a vacuum.
S|The speed of light in vacuum is a universal physical constant denoted c, exactly equal to 299792458 m/s.
S|It is exact because, by international agreement, a metre is defined as the length of the path travelled by light in vacuum during a time interval of 1/299792458 second.
S|The value 299,792,458 metres per second is approximately 1 billion kilometres per hour; 700 million miles per hour.
S|All forms of electromagnetic radiation, including visible light, travel in vacuum at the speed c as do massless particles and field perturbations, such as gravitational waves.
S|The speed of light in vacuum is the same for all observers, no matter their relative velocity.
S|As a result, massless particles and waves travel at c in a vacuum regardless of the motion of the source or the inertial reference frame of the observer.
S|The speed of light in vacuum is the upper limit for the speed at which information, matter, or energy can travel through space.
S|Particles with nonzero rest mass can be accelerated to approach c but can never reach it, regardless of the frame of reference in which their speed is measured.
S|Due to its finite speed, sunlight takes 8 minutes and 10 to 27 seconds to reach Earth, depending on the time of year.
S|The speed at which light propagates through transparent materials, such as glass or air, is less than c.
S|The ratio between c and the speed v at which light travels in a material is called the refractive index n of the material (n = c / v).
S|For example, for visible light, the refractive index of glass is typically around 1.5, meaning that light in glass travels at about 200000 km/s; the refractive index of air for visible light is about 1.0003.
S|Albert Einstein postulated that the speed of light c with respect to any inertial frame of reference is a constant and is independent of the motion of the light source.
S|He explored the consequences of that postulate by deriving the theory of relativity, and so showed that the parameter c had relevance outside of the context of light and electromagnetism.
S|In the theory of relativity, c interrelates space and time and appears in the famous mass-energy equivalence, E = mc^2.
S|The speed of light in vacuum is usually denoted by a lowercase c.
S|The constant c is defined in the International System of Units (SI) as exactly 299792458 m/s.
S|In imperial units, the speed of light is approximately 186282 miles per second.
S|Massless particles travel at the speed of light.
S|More generally, it is impossible for signals or energy to travel faster than c.

S|The speed of light, in any medium, which is usually denoted by c, is a physical constant important in many areas of physics.
S|It is denoted by "c0" especially in vacuum medium, although the symbol 'c' can be used to refer to that in any medium.
S|It is exactly 299,792,458 metres per second (983,571,056 feet per second) by definition.
S|A photon (particle of light) travels at this speed in a vacuum.
S|According to special relativity, c is the maximum speed at which all energy, matter, and physical information in the universe can travel.
S|It is the speed of all massless particles such as photons, and associated fields, including electromagnetic radiation such as light, in a vacuum.
S|It is predicted by the current theory to be the speed of gravity (that is, gravitational waves).
S|Such particles and waves travel at c regardless of the motion of the source or the inertial frame of reference of the observer.
S|In the theory of relativity, c interrelates space and time, and appears in the famous equation of mass-energy equivalence E = mc^2.
S|The special theory of relativity is based on the prediction, so far upheld by observations, that the measured speed of light in a vacuum is the same whether or not the source of the light and the person doing the measuring are moving relative to each other.
S|This is sometimes expressed as "the speed of light is independent of the reference frame." A consequence of this fact, that the speed of light is the same for all (inertial) observers, is that no matter how much energy is used, nothing with mass can be accelerated to reach or go faster than the speed of light.
S|Maxwell's equations predicted the speed of light and confirmed Michael Faraday's idea that light was an electromagnetic wave (a way that energy moves).
S|The index of refraction of a clear material is the ratio between the speed of light in a vacuum and the speed of light in that material.
S|The finite speed of light is a major constraint on long-distance space travel.
S|Supposing a journey to the other side of the Milky Way, the total time for a message and its reply would be about 200,000 years.
S|The speed of light can also be of concern over very short distances.
S|In supercomputers, the speed of light imposes a limit on how quickly data can be sent between processors.
S|If a processor operates at 1 gigahertz, a signal can only travel a maximum of about 30 centimetres (1 ft) in a single cycle.
