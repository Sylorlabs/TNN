S|# The Immune System (for Teens) - CHOC Childrens ## Overview Whether you're stomping through the showers in your bare feet after gym class or touching the bathroom doorknob, you're being exposed to germs.
S|Fortunately for most of us, the immune system is constantly on call to do battle with bugs that could put us out of commission.
S|### What Is the Immune System?
S|The immune system is the body's defense against infections.
S|The immune (pronounced: ih-MYOON) system attacks germs and helps keep us healthy.
S|### What Are the Parts of the Immune System?
S|Many cells and organs work together to protect the body.
S|White blood cells, also called **leukocytes** (pronounced: LOO-kuh-sytes), play an important role in the immune system.
S|Some types of white blood cells, called **phagocytes** (pronounced: FAH-guh-sytes), chew up invading organisms.
S|Others, called **lymphocytes** (pronounced: LIM-fuh-sytes), help the body remember the invaders and destroy them.
S|One type of phagocyte is the **neutrophil** (pronounced: NOO-truh-fil), which fights bacteria.
S|When someone might have bacterial infection, doctors can order a blood test to see if it caused the body to have lots of neutrophils.
S|Other types of phagocytes do their own jobs to make sure that the body responds to invaders.
S|The two kinds of lymphocytes are **B lymphocytes** and **T lymphocytes**.
S|Lymphocytes start out in the bone marrow and either stay there and mature into B cells, or go to the thymus gland to mature into T cells.
S|B lymphocytes are like the body's military intelligence system — they find their targets and send defenses to lock onto them.
S|T cells are like the soldiers — they destroy the invaders that the intelligence system finds.
S|## How Does the Immune System Work?
S|When the body senses foreign substances — germs like viruses or bacteria — the immune system works to recognize and get rid of them.
S|It does this with the help of **antigens,** which are “labels” on the substances that tell the immune system what’s harmful and what isn’t.
S|Germs (and even things like tumor cells and normal cells of the body) have antigens on them.
S|B lymphocytes are triggered to make **antibodies** (also called immunoglobulins).
S|These proteins lock onto specific antigens.
S|After they're made, antibodies usually stay in our bodies in case we have to fight the same germ again.
S|That's why someone who gets sick with a disease, like chickenpox, usually won't get sick from it again.
S|This is also how **immunizations** (vaccines) prevent some diseases.
S|An immunization introduces the body to an antigen in a way that doesn't make someone sick.
S|But it does let the body make antibodies that will protect the person from future attack by the germ.
S|Although antibodies can recognize an antigen and lock onto it, they can't destroy the germ without help.
S|That's the job of the **T cells**.
S|They destroy germs tagged by antibodies or cells that are infected or somehow changed.
S|(Some T cells are actually called "killer cells.") T cells also help signal other cells (like phagocytes) to do their jobs.
S|Antibodies also can: - **neutralize toxins** (poisonous or damaging substances) produced by different organisms - **activate a group of proteins called complement** that are part of the immune system.
S|(Complement helps kill bacteria, viruses, or infected cells.) These specialized cells and parts of the immune system offer the body protection against disease.
S|This protection is called immunity.
S|## Types of Immunity Humans have three types of immunity — innate, adaptive, and passive: - **Innate immunity:** Everyone is born with innate (or natural) immunity, a type of general protection.
S|For example, the skin acts as a barrier to block germs from entering the body.
S|And the immune system recognizes when some invaders are foreign and could be dangerous.
S|- **Adaptive immunity:** Adaptive (or active) immunity develops throughout our lives.
S|We develop adaptive immunity when we're exposed to diseases or when we're immunized against them with vaccines.
S|- **Passive immunity:** Passive immunity is "borrowed" from another source and it lasts for a short time.
S|For example, antibodies in a mother's breast milk give a baby temporary immunity to diseases the mother has been exposed to.
S|**The immune system needs help from vaccines.
S|Getting all your recommended vaccines on time can help keep you as healthy as possible.** So can washing your hands well and often to avoid infection, eating right, getting plenty of and, and going for regular medical checkups.

S|title: The Immune System | Health | Biology | FuseSchool description: The main role of the immune system is to prevent disease caused by infection.
S|Infections can be caused by a wide variety of pathogens, including bacteria, fungi, parasites (such as malaria) and viruses (such as influenza and the common cold).
S|The immune system comprises of a network of cells and tissues that work together to prevent disease.
S|All parts of the body that interact with the external environment (such as the lungs, digestive tract and skin) have to be prepared to protect the body.
S|The skin acts as a barrier to prevent pathogens entering the body.
S|If you cut your leg, your skin protective barrier is broken and so bacteria are now able to enter your cut.
S|Luckily, our bodies have an army of immune cells waiting for external invaders.
S|These immune cells are called macrophages.
S|They are able to recognise the bacteria as a threat, based on molecules the bacteria express on their surfaces.
S|Once the macrophages recognised the bacteria, they can destroy the bacteria by eating (engulfing) it.
S|This is a process called phagocytosis.
S|At the same time, the phagocytes produce molecules called cytokines and chemokines.
S|These alert the rest of the immune system to the presence of an invader.
S|Different immune cells called neutrophils are then recruited to your leg.
S|Along with the macrophages, they help to destroy the bacteria.
S|Both the macrophages and neutrophils are part of the innate immune system.
S|They provide the first line of defence against invading pathogens.
S|If the invaders cannot be controlled by the innate immune system, then more specialised immune cells called lymphocytes are recruited.
S|Lymphocytes include T-cells and B-cells.
S|They work together to produce molecules like antibodies, which help to clear the infection.
S|T and B cells are part of the adaptive immune system.
S|Once these cells are activated, they are able to remember an infection.
S|So that the next time the bacteria enters the body, they are able to be destroyed more rapidly.
S|The innate and adaptive immune systems work together to fight off infections, and prevent the development of disease.
