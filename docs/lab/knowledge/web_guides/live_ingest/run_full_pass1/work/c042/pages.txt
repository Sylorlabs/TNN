S|[Image 0: Illustration of a cartoon robot, the mascot of NASA Space Place.] There are a few different definitions of **constellations**, but many people think of constellations as a group of stars.
S|Often, it's a group of stars that looks like a particular shape in the sky and has been given a name.
S|These stars are far away from Earth.
S|They are not connected to each other at all.
S|Some stars in a constellation might be close while others are very far away.
S|But, if you were to draw lines in the sky between the stars like a dot-to-dot puzzle – and use lots of imagination – the picture would look like an object, animal, or person.
S|Over time, cultures around the world have had different names and numbers of constellations depending on what people thought they saw.
S|Today, there are 88 officially recognized constellations.
S|[Image 1: A group of stars with a line traced through them to form the Big Dipper.] This group of stars is called the "big dipper." If you trace a line between the stars, it looks like a ladle, or dipper, that you'd use to dip soup from a pot.
S|This photo of the big dipper was taken by an astronaut on the International Space Station, but you can often see this group of stars from the ground, too!

S|Pettit ## What constellations can you see in the night sky?
S|The constellations you can see at night depend on the time of year.
S|Earth orbits around the Sun once each year.
S|Our view into space through the night sky changes as we orbit.
S|So, the night sky looks slightly different each night because Earth is in a different spot in its orbit.
S|The stars appear each night to move slightly west of where they were the night before.
S|Your location on Earth also determines what stars and constellations you see, and how high they appear to rise in the sky.
S|The Northern Hemisphere is always pointing in a different direction than the Southern Hemisphere.
S|This means that stargazers in Australia, for example, get a slightly different view of the sky and can see a few different constellations than those in the United States.
S|It can be a little confusing to picture how the night sky changes as we orbit the Sun.
S|You can see how it all works in the illustration below.
S|[Image 2: Chart showing some of the constellations that are visible from the Northern Hemisphere in different times of year.] A chart showing some of the constellations that are visible from the Northern Hemisphere in different times of year.
S|Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech For example, say you’re in the Northern Hemisphere looking into the night sky on September 21.
S|You’ll probably be able to see the constellation Pisces.
S|But you won’t see Virgo because that constellation is on the other side of the Sun.
S|During that time of year, Virgo’s stars would only be visible during the daytime – but you’d never see them because of the brightness of our Sun.
S|Our star finder activity is a great way to have some fun and learn about the constellations visible in our night sky each month.

S|[Image 3: Image of the Orion nebula captured by the Hubble Space Telescope.] This image of the Orion nebula was captured by the Hubble Space Telescope.
S|It is located in the constellation Orion, below the three stars that form the hunter's belt.

S|Robberto *Astronomy* is the scientific study of everything in outer space.
S|Astronomers and other scientists study stars and galaxies, most of which are many light-years away from Earth.
S|Studying the scientific properties of these objects in space helps us to understand how the universe was made, what else is out there, and how we fit in.
S|*Astrology* is not the same thing as astronomy.
S|As a science, astronomy follows the scientific process involving evidence and data.
S|Astrology is based on the belief that the location of certain stars and planets in the sky can predict the future or describe what a person is like.
S|While astrology is important to some cultural traditions, its claims are not based on scientific evidence.
S|## How does NASA use the constellations?
S|[Image 4: Photo of the constellation Orion taken by NASA astronaut Karen Nyberg aboard the International Space Station.] This photo of the constellation Orion was taken by NASA astronaut Karen Nyberg aboard the International Space Station.
S|Credit: NASA/Karen Nyberg Stars and constellations stay in approximately the same spot for many, many years.
S|They only appear to move in the sky during the year because we are on a moving planet.
S|Because the constellations are in a fixed location, they are often used as landmarks in the sky.
S|Many stars, nebulae, and other objects are named after the constellations they are found in.
S|For example, meteor showers are named for the constellation where the meteors appear to be coming from.
S|So, for example, the Orionids meteor shower, which occurs in October each year, appear to come from the same direction as the constellation Orion the Hunter.
S|[Image 5: Photo of the constellation Orion framed by two Perseid meteors.] The constellation Orion is framed by two Perseid meteors on Aug.
S|12, 2018, in Cedar Breaks National Monument, Utah.
S|The Perseid meteor showers appear to come from the same direction as the constellation Perseus.
S|Credit: NASA/Bill Dunford Known stars, such as those in well-known constellations, can also be used to navigate.
S|For centuries, sailors used stars to determine their location when out at sea.
S|This is called celestial navigation.
S|NASA astronauts have also trained to use celestial navigation as a backup in case modern navigation systems have trouble.
S|https://www.youtube.com/embed/LVqjvvLO5XY?rel=0 Watch this video to learn how astronauts on the International Space Station are testing this old method of celestial navigation!
S|Robotic spacecraft also use maps of the stars to find their way.
S|They carry a star map in their onboard computers and compare these star maps to patterns of stars in images they take.
S|So, in a way, patterns of stars are as helpful today as they were to ancient navigators.

S|# constellation - Kids | Britannica Kids | Homework Help Interactive A constellation is a group of stars.
S|The groups are identified according to patterns that people have seen in the stars.
S|For example, the stars of the constellation Leo seem to trace the outline of a lion.
S|The name Leo means “lion” in the Latin language.
S|The constellation Draco, whose name is Latin for “dragon,” is said to look like a dragon.
S|However, these are not scientific groupings.
S|They are simply ways that people have imagined the stars.
S|Since ancient times people have tried to explain the night sky.
S|Over thousands of years different cultures have seen different patterns in the stars.
S|They have named many different constellations after familiar animals, everyday objects, and characters and beasts from stories.
S|Today constellations provide a connection between modern humans and ancient stories.
S|They are also interesting to spot in the night sky.
S|Astronomers, or scientists who study stars, have named 88 constellations.
S|They use the constellations to help describe the location of specific stars.
S|Among them are the 12 well-known constellations that lie in a band of space called the zodiac.
S|These are Aries, Taurus, Gemini, Cancer, Leo, Virgo, Libra, Scorpius, Sagittarius, Capricornus, Aquarius, and Pisces.
S|A person looking at the sky from the same point every night will see different constellations at different times of year.
S|This happens because Earth moves around the Sun, while the stars do not.
S|Earth completes one trip around the Sun each year, so different stars are visible from Earth in the course of a year.

S|# Kids.Net.Au - Encyclopedia > Constellation A **constellation** is a group of stars visibly related to each other in a particular configuration.
S|In three-dimensional space, most of the stars we see have little relation to one another, but can appear to be grouped on the imaginary plane of the night sky.
S|Humans excel at finding patterns and throughout history have grouped stars that appear close to one another into constellations.
S|The grouping of stars into contellations is essentially arbitrary, and different cultures have had different constellations, although a few of the more obvious ones tend to recur frequently, e.g.
S|The International Astronomical Union (IAU) divides the sky into 88 official constellations with precise boundaries, so that every direction belongs to exactly one constellation.
S|These are mostly based upon the constellations of the ancient Greek tradition, passed down through the Middle Ages.
S|Various other less official patterns have existed alongside the constellations called asterisms, such as the Big Dipper and Little Dipper.
S|Note that in any of these figures the stars rarely have anything to do with one another - they are just along roughly the same lines of sight, and are typically very far apart.
S|**Star names** Many stars are named using the genitive of the constellation in which they are found.
S|These names include Bayer designations such as Alpha Centauri, Flamsteed designations such as 61 Cygni, and variable star designations such as RR Lyrae.
S|For more information about star names, see Star designations.
