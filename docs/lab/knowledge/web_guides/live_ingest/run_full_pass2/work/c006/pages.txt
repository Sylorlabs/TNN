P|c006-p1|What is the speed of light? | Live Science
H|www.livescience.com
S|The universe has a speed limit, and it's the speed of light.
S|Nothing can travel faster than light — not even our best spacecraft — according to the laws of physics.
S|So, what is the speed of light?
S|Light moves at an incredible 186,000 miles per second (300,000 kilometers per second), equivalent to almost 700 million mph (more than 1 billion km/h).
S|That's fast enough to circumnavigate the globe 7.5 times in one second, while a typical passenger jet would take more than two days to go around once (and that doesn't include stops for fuel or layovers!).
S|Light moves so fast that, for much of human history, we thought it traveled instantaneously.
S|As early as the late 1600s, though, scientist Ole Roemer was able to measure the speed of light (usually referred to as *c*) by using observations of Jupiter's moons, according to Britannica.
S|Light doesn't always have to go so fast, though.
S|Depending on what it's traveling through — air, water, diamonds, etc.
S|— it can slow down.
S|The official speed of light is measured as if it's traveling in a vacuum, a space with no air or anything to get in the way.
S|You can most clearly see differences in the speed of light in something like a prism, where certain energies of light bend more than others, creating a rainbow.
S|Interestingly, the speed of light is no match for the vast distances of space, which is itself a vacuum.
S|It takes 8 minutes for light from the sun to reach Earth, and a couple years for light from the other closest stars (like Proxima Centauri) to get to our planet.
S|This is why astronomers use the unit light-years — the distance light can travel in one year — to measure vast distances in space.
P|c006-p2|Speed of light
H|simple.wikipedia.org
S|The **speed of light**, in any medium, which is usually denoted by c, is a physical constant important in many areas of physics.
S|It is denoted by "c0" especially in vacuum medium, although the symbol 'c' can be used to refer to that in any medium.
S|It is exactly 299,792,458 metres per second (983,571,056 feet per second) by definition.
S|A photon (particle of light) travels at this speed in a vacuum.
S|According to special relativity, c is the maximum speed at which all energy, matter, and physical information in the universe can travel.
S|It is the speed of all massless particles such as photons, and associated fields—including electromagnetic radiation such as light—in a vacuum.
S|In the theory of relativity, c interrelates space and time, and appears in the famous equation of mass–energy equivalence *E* = *mc*2.
S|The special theory of relativity is based on the prediction, so far upheld by observations, that the measured speed of light in a vacuum is the same whether or not the source of the light and the person doing the measuring are moving relative to each other.
S|This is sometimes expressed as "the speed of light is independent of the reference frame." ## Example explaining how speed does not depend on reference frame George is standing on the ground next to some train tracks (railroad).
S|There is a train rushing by at 30 mph (48 km/h).
S|George throws a baseball at 90 mph (140 km/h) in the direction the train is moving.
S|Tom, a passenger on the train, has a device (like a radar gun) to measure throwing speeds.
S|Because he is on the train, Tom is already moving at 30 mph (48 km/h) in the direction of the throw, so Tom measures the speed of the ball as only 60 mph (97 km/h).
S|Now, repeat the experiment with light instead of a baseball; that is, George has a flashlight instead of throwing a baseball.
S|George is standing on the ground next to some train tracks.
S|There is a train rushing by at 1/3 the speed of light.
S|George flashes a light beam in the direction the train is moving.
S|George measures the speed of light as 186,282 miles per second (299,792 kilometres per second).
S|Tom, a passenger on the train, measures the speed of the light beam.
S|It sounds impossible, but that is what one measures: the full value, 186,282 miles per second (299,792 kilometres per second).
S|A consequence of this fact, that the speed of light is the same for all (inertial) observers, is that no matter how much energy is used, nothing with mass can be accelerated to reach or go faster than the speed of light.

S|And yet, according to Einstein’s theory of special relativity, no object with mass can ever travel faster than the speed of light as this would require an infinite amount of energy, which is clearly not possible.

S|However, unlocking this energy would require an equivalent amount of antimatter, which when it annihilates with matter releases its stored mass energy.
