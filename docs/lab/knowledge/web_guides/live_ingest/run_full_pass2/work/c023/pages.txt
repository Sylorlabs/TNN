S|# Human eyes: How they work, and their amazing anatomy | BBC Science Focus Containing more than 100 million light-sensitive cells and some of the fastest muscles in our bodies, our eyes are capable of picking up everything from starlight beaming across the galaxy to the full gamut of human emotion.
S|But it’s in their coupling with our minds – through the approximately one million nerve fibres connecting each eye to our brains – that we gain the ability to synthesise slick visuals and interpret what’s going on around us without getting distracted or overwhelmed.
S|## Key parts of the human eye ### **Pupil** It looks like a black dot but it’s actually a gap that lets light pass through to the back of your eye.
S|### **Cornea** A transparent dome at the front of your eye, the cornea refracts light, helping to direct it along the right path to the retina.
S|### **Iris** The coloured part of the eye that controls how much light gets in by causing the pupil to dilate or constrict.
S|The iris is part of a larger structure that forms a layer between the retina and sclera.
S|### **Lens** A transparent lens that changes shape to focus incoming light on the retina.
S|### **Retina** Often thought of as the back of the eye, it’s more correctly the innermost layer that the light hits after being inverted by the lens.
S|The retina is jam-packed with light-sensitive cells and nerve cells.
S|### **Sclera** Otherwise known as the white of the eye, the sclera is a protective, outer layer made tough by collagen fibres.
S|### **Viterous** A transparent jelly that fills the eyeball.
S|As we get older, the consistency of this jelly becomes more liquid and progressively less wobbly.
S|### **Optic nerve** A thick bundle of nerve fibres carrying signals between each eye and the brain.
S|## How do we see?
S|The simplest explanation is that what we see is a result of light entering the eyes through the cornea and lens, which direct and focus the light towards the photosensitive cells (rods and cones) in the retina.
S|Rods work in low light, while cones require bright light but give us colour vision.
S|Cones provide the details at the centre of our vision, while rods are responsible for peripheral vision.
S|Together, these cells convert light into electric signals, which travel down the optic nerve to the brain.
S|What we actually see, though, is as much about how our brains interpret these signals so that we can make sense of the information we’re receiving without being distracted by unimportant details.
S|This visual prioritisation and filtering produce a ‘mental image’ that is only a representation of what is actually there.
S|For example, our brains prioritise faces, with the result that we’re always spotting them where they don’t exist – such as in clouds and wallpaper patterns.
S|Our brains are also constantly processing what we see to provide a smooth visual experience, rather than giving us the effect of a shaky, handheld camera.
S|Recent research suggests it does this by blending together the inputs from the previous 10-15 seconds, meaning what we see isn’t necessarily a second-bysecond update, but it does give us a less hectic sense of the world.
S|## How do our eyes compare to cameras?
S|It’s hard to compare because what we see isn’t made up of pixels or frames, and we’re constantly switching where we focus.
S|It’s been suggested that our eyes have the resolution (ability to distinguish two points) of a 576-megapixel camera.
S|A stills camera capable of capturing 576 million separate pixels within a single image would be five times better than some of the highest resolution cameras currently on the market.
S|However, our eyes only see with very high resolution at the centre of our vision.
S|So, everywhere else, the resolution is much more comparable to a standard camera.
S|As for colours, we can only see those that fall into the small range of wavelengths visible to humans (400-700 nanometers), whereas we’ve built cameras that are capable of perceiving the shorter wavelengths of ultraviolet light (10-400 nm) and the longer wavelengths of infrared (750-15000 nm).
S|Within the visible spectrum, most people are thought to be able to discriminate around 10 million different shades using three types of cone cells.
S|This system is not dissimilar to that of a camera, which combines red, blue and green pixels to capture nearly 17 million colours.
S|But, people with tetrachromacy have a genetic mutation that gives them an extra cone type, allowing them to see 100 times more colours, like birds and reptiles.
S|## How does ageing affect our eyes?
S|Our vision deteriorates as we get older.
S|In middle age, the lenses in our eyes become less flexible, making it harder to focus close-up, which is why many people start needing glasses for reading in their 40s and 50s.
S|A number of age-related conditions are also leading causes of blindness.

S|## Why do people have different eye colours?
S|Recent research suggests there are at least – and that’s just in European and Asian people.
S|This means that, contrary to what we might have been taught at school, it’s what colour eyes a child born to two blue-eyed parents should have.
S|(It’s quite possible, although less likely, for them to have brown eyes.) But what is it in the iris that makes it blue or brown?
S|Well, dark irises contain more of the naturally brown, light-absorbing pigment melanin – the same pigment that gives our skin different colours.
S|Blue irises that contain less melanin absorb less light and instead reflect and scatter it, producing short wavelengths of light at the blue end of the spectrum.
S|Differences in melanin levels also explain why some people have two different-coloured irises (heterochromia), usually the result of a harmless genetic mutation affecting melanin development in the eye.
S|Meanwhile, in babies whose eyes are blue but later turn brown, their melanin is still forming.
S|In fact, babies were once the only humans with blue eyes, because the genetic mutation responsible for adults having blue eyes is not thought to have appeared in European populations until the last 6,000-10,000 years.

S|# Eye anatomy and function An overview of how the many parts of the eye work together to produce clear vision.
S|## The anatomy of the eye The eye has many parts that must work together to produce clear vision: - The sclera, or white part of the eye, protects the eyeball.
S|- The pupil, or black dot at the centre of the eye, is an opening through which light can enter the eye.
S|- The iris, or coloured part of the eye, surrounds the pupil.
S|It controls how much light enters the eye by changing the size of the pupil.
S|- The cornea, a clear window at the front of the eye, covers the iris and the pupil.
S|- A clear lens, located behind the pupil, acts like a camera lens by focusing light onto the retina at the back of the eye.
S|- The retina is a light-sensitive inner lining at the back of the eye.
S|Ten different layers of cells work together in the retina to detect light and turn it into electrical impulses.Anatomy of the eye: 3/4 view [Image 0: Diagram of parts of the eye: optic nerve, optic desk, retina, macula, lens, iris, pupil, cornea and sclera]Illustration shows the right eyeball with a section removed to see the interior structures.
S|## The retina Special cells called cones and rods are located in the retina.
S|These cells are known as photoreceptors and help absorb light.
S|### Cones The majority of the cones are located in the macula, or central area, of the retina.
S|Cone cells help us see colour and detail.
S|Similarly, the macula allows us to read and clearly recognize people's facial details, such as eye colour and whether they have freckles.
S|### Rods The majority of the rods are located in the peripheral, or outer area, of the retina.
S|Rod cells allow us to see in poor lighting and give us our night vision.
S|## How the eye sees For people with normally functioning eyes, the following sequence takes place: 1.
S|Light reflects off the object we are looking at.
S|Light rays enter the eye through the cornea at the front of the eye.
S|The light passes through a watery fluid (aqueous humor), and enters the pupil to reach the lens.
S|The lens can change in thickness to bend the light, which will focus it onto the retina at the back of the eye.
S|On the way to the retina, the light passes through a thick, clear fluid called a vitreous humor.
S|The vitreous humor fills the eyeball and helps maintain its round shape.
S|The light then reaches the back of the eye and hits the retina.
S|The retina translates the light into electrical impulses which are then carried to the brain by the optic nerve.
S|Finally, the visual cortex (or centre) of the brain interprets these impulses as what we see.
S|## What normal vision is like To understand the vision of someone with an eye condition, it can be helpful to know what normal vision is like.
S|Imagine a scenario where two people are sitting on the couch in front of you.
S|If you look directly at Person A, you are able to use your macula, or central vision, to see the details of their head and face.
S|Maybe they have freckles, brown eyes and black hair.
S|At the same time, you are aware that Person B is sitting on the couch beside Person A.
S|However, you are not able to see the same amount of detail on their face.
S|For example, you may only see dark areas where their eyes are.
S|To see Person B, you are using the rest of your retina or peripheral vision.
S|Seeing clearly and sharply in the centre, and blurry in the periphery is considered normal vision.
S|## Vision problems A problem with any part of the eye can cause vision problems.
S|There are many types of eye conditions that can affect vision in different ways.
S|In some cases, the lens does not focus correctly, or the shape of the eyeball is not round, so the image will appear blurry.
S|This can often be corrected with prescription glasses or contact lenses.
S|When an image is focused behind the retina, this is referred to as far-sightedness.
S|When an image is focused in front of the retina, this is referred to as near-sightedness (myopia).
S|Some eye conditions affect the retina.
S|For some people, only their peripheral vision is affected, which can cause tunnel vision.
S|For others, only their central vision is affected, which can lead to the formation of blind spots (scotomas).
S|Finally, other eye conditions that may cause vision problems include cloudiness in the lens (cataract), increased eye pressure (glaucoma), damage to the cornea, or problems with the eye muscles.
S|## Visual field (VF) Visual field (VF) is the term used to describe the width of our peripheral vision.
S|Normally, we see with a VF of 180 degrees or half a circle.
S|When looking straight ahead, we should be able to sense motion off our right or left shoulder.
S|### How VF is measured Visual field can be measured in different ways.
S|Normally, VF is measured using an automated machine called a Humphrey Visual Field Analyzer.
S|Occasionally, a manual test called a Goldmann VF exam is used.
S|A person’s VF is measured by asking them to focus their eyes straight ahead.
S|Next, a light source is brought into their field of vision from the side.
S|The person then pushes a button when they see the light.
S|The results of the VF test determine whether a person has tunnel vision and blind spots.
S|Visual fields can be measured in one eye at a time or using both eyes at once (binocular fields).
S|A person with blind spots in both eyes may need to have a binocular visual field test to make sure that one eye sees in an area that is a blind spot in the other.
S|This is important information when determining whether someone has a VF that meets the government standards for driving (see below).
S|## Visual acuity (VA) Visual acuity (VA) is defined as the clarity of the image seen by the eye.
S|VA is tested using an eye chart at a distance of 20 feet (six metres).
S|From testing many patients, eye doctors have determined what the average VA is for most people when standing 20 feet away from an eye chart.
S|This measurement is called "normal" vision.
S|Young children who are unable to read the letters on the chart will have their vision checked using preferential looking tests where cards with lines or pictures are held up for a child to look at.
S|The child does not need to verbally respond but will often look to the side (left or right, up or down) of the card with the picture, which will indicate the child can see it.
S|The cards will get progressively more difficult to see, and the test continues until the child stops responding.
S|The cards are graded and the level of vision on the 20/20 scale can be estimated.
S|Normal vision is 20/20 VA.
S|In metric units, it is called 6/6 VA because 20 feet is equal to six metres.
S|A VA of 20/20 means that when standing 20 feet away from the eye chart, a normal sighted person can see line 20 clearly.
S|A person with poor VA may be able to use corrective lenses (glasses or contact lenses) to correct their vision to 20/20.
S|If corrective lenses cannot correct vision to 20/20, or at least 20/50, then the person's vision is considered too blurry to drive a car.
S|### Examples of visual acuity A VA of 20/50 means that a person standing 20 feet away from the eye chart will see what a 20/20 sighted person can see when standing 50 feet away.
S|So, a person with 20/50 vision would need to stand 30 feet closer than a 20/20 sighted person to see the same amount of detail.
S|A person with a VA of 20/200 is able to read the biggest letter on an eye chart (usually the letter E), but all the other lines on the chart are blurry.
S|A VA of 20/200 means that a person needs to be 20 feet from an eye chart to see the same amount of detail that a 20/20 sighted person can see from 200 feet away.
S|It should be noted that 20/20 VA does not always mean perfect vision.
S|It is average vision.
S|There are other vision skills that contribute to overall visual ability.
S|These include: - peripheral awareness or side vision - eye co-ordination - depth perception - ability to focus - colour vision ## Definitions of "legally blind" The term “legally blind” has a few different meanings.
S|It is important that you ask your optometrist or ophthalmologist what the standard definition of “legally blind” is in your province, state or country.
S|## Driving To drive legally with a private G class license in Ontario, a person must have a VA of at least 20/50, with both eyes open with corrective lenses.
S|The person's VF must be at least 120 degrees and not interrupted by blind spots.
S|The VF must also be at least 15 degrees above and below the fixation point with both eyes open.
S|A person can have their vision classified as “legally blind” for driving when their VA or VF does not meet the guidelines set by the Ministry of Transportation (MOT).
S|An ophthalmologist is required by law to report any patient that does not meet the MOT guidelines.
S|## Assistive devices program (ADP) To qualify for funding for low vision aids through the Assistive Devices Program (ADP) in Ontario, a person must have a VA of 20/70 or worse with their best correction.
S|Such a person is classified as "legally blind for assistive devices." These people should be seen by a low vision specialist, or licensed doctor of optometry, to learn about which low vision aids are available and appropriate for their vision problems.
S|Low vision aids include hand-held magnifiers, magnification in reading glasses and telescopes.
S|## Disability tax credit and Registered Disability Savings Plan To be eligible for the Disability Tax Credit Certificate in Canada, a person must have a VA of 20/200 or worse or a VF of less than 20 degrees.
S|The Disability Tax Credit Certificate (form T2201) is available at.
S|If eligible, a person is entitled to a Registered Disability Savings Plan (RDSP).
S|With the RDSP, the government matches your contribution up to a maximum of $3,000.
S|Ask your bank for details.

S|## COMMON EYE PROBLEMS With such a complex structure, the eye can experience a number of problems and disorders that affect your ability to see.
S|Causes for these disorders range from ageing to infection to damage to the eye’s structure or nerves.
S|Below are the top 10 most common eye disorders, along with information about their causes and treatment.
S|### AGE-RELATED MACULAR DEGENERATION **Causes**: Damage to the retina’s macula, which is responsible for the central, high-resolution, colour vision that is possible in good light.
S|**Symptoms**: Blurred vision, or lack of vision, in the centre of the visual field.
S|**Treatment**: There are several treatment types: Anti-VEGF (Anti–vascular endothelial growth factor) medication injected into the eye, laser coagulation and photodynamic therapy.
S|### **BLEPHARITIS** **Causes**: Typically caused by bacterial infection.
S|**Symptoms**: Chronic inflammation of the eyelid, usually at the base of the eyelashes.This results in scales and debris which can irritate and cause an itching or burning sensation of the eye.
S|**Treatment**: Routine eyelid hygiene, such as a warm compress, eyelid massage, and eyelid scrubs.
S|Some ophthalmologists and optometrists may prescribe a low-dose antibiotic.
S|### **CATARACTS** **Causes**: Cataracts can be brought on in myriad ways – age, blunt trauma and skin diseases are commonly seen causes, as are smoking, alcohol abuse, even radiation exposure.
S|**Symptoms**: Your vision may be impacted in several ways, including faded colours , blurry vision, trouble with bright lights, trouble seeing at night, and halos appearing around brighter light sources.
S|**Treatment**: Cataract surgery, sometimes also referred to as lens replacement surgery.
S|### **GLAUCOMA** **Causes**: One of the most important risk factors in glaucoma is increased pressure within the eye, known as ocular hypertension.
S|Other elements, such as dietary habits, genetics and ethnicity, are sometimes also factors.
S|**Symptoms**: Loss of peripheral vision at first, sometimes followed by loss of central vision and potentially blindness if left untreated.
S|**Treatment**: Laser surgery, traditional surgery and medication are all options for treatment of glaucoma.
S|### **AMBLYOPIA** **Causes**: Amblyopia has three main causes: - Strabismic; caused by misaligned eyes - Refractive; due to refractive error in one or both eyes - Deprivational; brought on by deprivation of vision in early life from vision obstructing disorders.
S|**Symptoms**: Often not noticed until later in life, symptoms include poor pattern recognition, abnormalities in spatial vision, and reduction in visual acuity and contrast sensitivity.
S|**Treatment**: Usually treated by wearing spectacles to correct the vision deficit in the affected eye, it’s also sometimes treated by topical application of the good eye in order to force use of the amblyopic eye.
S|### **RETINAL DETACHMENT OR TEAR** **Causes**: Retina separates from the layer of blood vessels beneath it.
S|**Symptoms**: Retinal detachment, or Retinal tears result in an increased number of ‘floaters’, flashes of light, and worsening of the outer section of the visual field.
S|**Treatment**: Treatment is often urgently needed for this condition, and is possible through a number of techniques: laser photocoagulation, scleral buckle surgery and pneumatic retinopexy.
S|### **DIABETIC EYE DISEASE / DIABETIC RETINOPATHY** **Causes****:** Brought on by the damage that diabetes mellitus causes to the blood vessels in the tissue at the back of the eye (retina).
S|**Symptoms**: Early symptoms include floaters, blurriness and dark areas of vision.
S|Blindness can sometimes occur.
S|**Treatment**: Mild cases can be treated through the careful management of diabetes, while more severe, advanced cases require surgery or laser surgery.
S|### **DRY EYE SYNDROME** **Causes**: Chronic lack of sufficient lubrication and moisture on the surface of the eye **Symptoms**: The main symptom of course is dryness of the eyes, but this can lead to constant eye irritation and significant inflammation and sometimes even eye scarring.
S|**Treatment**: Often, the routine application of artificial tears to the eye is sufficient treatment.
S|In more severe cases, an ophthalmologist may recommend other eye medications to help your body to create and secrete more tears.
