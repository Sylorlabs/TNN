S|# Explainer: What is a neuron?
S|These sensory cells process information and move it, allowing you to do everything you do [Image 0: an illustration of a network of neurons and a brain] An artist’s drawing of your brain hosting a network of neurons that receives and passes along sensory information.
S|Nerve cells do important work throughout the body.
S|PIXOLOGICSTUDIO/SCIENCE PHOTO LIBRARY/Science Photo Library/Getty Images Plus It’s morning.
S|As you sit up in bed, your feet touch the cold floor, so you lift them and put on your socks.
S|In the kitchen, you watch the cereal pour from the box and hear it ping against the bowl.
S|You tip in a stream of milk — carefully — because you spilled it yesterday.
S|All of these experiences are possible because of cells in your brain, called neurons.
S|These cells are dedicated to sensing information in the world around you, then helping you respond to it and learn.
S|This family of cells send messages to each other, day and night.
S|Along the way, they sense information.
S|They tell other cells what to do.
S|And they remember and respond to what you have learned.
S|For instance, the smell of burning bread will trigger sensory neurons to to your brain.
S|This neurotransmission then informs motor neurons in your legs and arm muscles to run to the toaster and pop up the smoking toast.
S|Next time you use the appliance, you remember to turn down the heat, because some specialized neurons in your brain have connected to other neurons dedicated to memory.
S|Sensory and motor neurons are two different classes of neurons.
S|Within these classes are hundreds of different types, each built differently to do a specific job.
S|How all these neurons connect to each other changes from one person to another.
S|That’s what makes each of us unique in how we think, feel and act.
S|## What makes these cells special Neurons have all of the basic features of animal cells.
S|For instance, they have a and an outer membrane.
S|But unlike other cells, they also have branching hair-like structures called dendrites.
S|These catch chemical messages from other cells.
S|The dendrites send each impulse to the main part of the cell.
S|It’s known as the cell body.
S|From there, the signal moves along a long thin section of the cell called the axon.
S|This electrical impulse is made by waves of charged particles weaving in and out of the cell membrane, rippling the signal along.
S|Some axons have fatty rings of myelin (MY-eh-lin) on them, lined up like beads on a string.
S|When the neurons are myelinated, the message will bounce along much faster.
S|The message leaves an axon through finger-like terminals at the end.
S|Chemicals released out of the cell here will then be picked up by the dendrites on a neighboring cell.
S|The area from one cell’s terminals, across the gap between cells and on to the next cell’s dendrites is known as a synapse (SIH-napse).
S|Messages pass between one cell and onto the next by floating across the space between — a gap called the synaptic cleft.
S|This tiny space between the two cells is filled with fluid.
S|In the next neuron, the chemical signals enter molecules called receptors like a key into a lock.
S|## Anatomy of a Neuron Dendrites branch out from the head (cell body) of a neuron.
S|They receive chemicals which serve as a message.
S|When one arrives, it moves into the cell body.
S|From there, it travels as an electrical impulse down the axon to its terminals.
S|Those terminals will release packets of chemical messengers, passing on the signal to a neighboring neuron’s dendrites.
S|[Image 1: the anatomy of a neuron]Vitalii Dumma/iStock/Getty Images Plus Neurons in your brain relay messages across synapses and on through chains and webs of additional cells.
S|They transmit messages in much the same way that data move from computer to computer through the internet.
S|Scientists who study the brain — neuroscientists – work to understand the connections and messaging between neurons.
S|They use wires and magnets outside or inside the body to measure signals that pass through nerve cells.
S|This works because the messages are, molecules with positive and negative electrical charges.
S|The fluid inside and between all those neurons is made of these charged chemicals.
S|Neighboring neurons may not always be close by.
S|In the body, a single nerve cell can extend a pretty long axon — up to the length of your leg.
S|Your brain and spinal cord, however, are masses of branching networks of small neurons.
S|They have the support of other cells called.
S|Glial cells protect, support, feed and do cleanup for the neurons.
S|Think of them as the support crew for neurons.
S|Many cells in your body are replaced daily, such as stomach and skin cells.
S|But neurons live a long time.
S|In many cases, they are as old as you are.
S|Scientists are still figuring out when and where neurons first appear as your body develops.
S|They know they form from areas in the body rich with super-powered cells, called.
S|After neurons develop, they travel to different positions and start connecting to form networks.

S|# Neurons: What are they and how do they work?
S|Neurons are responsible for carrying information throughout the human body.
S|Using electrical and chemical signals, they help coordinate all of the necessary functions of life.
S|In short, our nervous systems detect what is going on around us and inside of us; they decide how we should act, alter the state of internal organs (heart rate changes, for instance), and allows us to think about and remember what is going on.
S|To do this, it relies on a sophisticated network — neurons.
S|It has been estimated that there are around neurons in the brain; to reach this huge target, a developing fetus must create around .
S|Each neuron is connected to another 1,000 neurons, creating an incredibly complex network of communication.
S|Neurons are considered the basic units of the nervous system.
S|Because they are Neurons, sometimes called nerve cells, make up around 10 percent of the brain; the rest consists of glial cells and astrocytes that support and nourish neurons.
S|Neurons can only be seen using a microscope and can be split into three parts: **Soma (cell body)** — this portion of the neuron receives information.
S|It contains the cell’s nucleus.
S|**Dendrites** — these thin filaments carry information from other neurons to the soma.
S|They are the “input” part of the cell.
S|**Axon** — this long projection carries information from the soma and sends it off to other cells.
S|This is the “output” part of the cell.
S|It normally ends with a number of synapses connecting to the dendrites of other neurons.
S|Both dendrites and axons are sometimes referred to as nerve fibers.
S|Axons vary in length a great deal.
S|Some can be tiny, whereas others can be over 1 meter long.
S|The longest axon is called the (DRG), a cluster of nerve cell bodies that carries information from the skin to the brain.
S|Some of the axons in the DRG travel from the toes to the brain stem — up to 2 meters in a tall person.
S|Neurons can be split into types in different ways, for instance, by connection or function.
S|### Connection **Efferent neurons** — these take messages from the (brain and spinal cord) and deliver them to cells in other parts of the body.
S|**Afferent neurons** — take messages from the rest of the body and deliver them to the central nervous system (CNS).
S|**Interneurons** — these relay messages between neurons in the CNS.
S|### Function **Sensory** — carry signals from the senses to the CNS.
S|**Relay** — carry signals from one place to another within the CNS.
S|**Motor** — carry signals from the CNS to muscles.
S|If a neuron receives a large number of inputs from other neurons, these signals add up until they exceed a particular threshold.
S|Once this threshold is exceeded, the neuron is triggered to send an impulse along its axon — this is called an action potential.
S|An action potential is created by the movement of electrically charged atoms (ions) across the axon’s membrane.
S|Neurons at rest are more negatively charged than the fluid that surrounds them; this is referred to as the membrane potential.
S|It is usually -70 millivolts (mV).
S|When the cell body of a nerve receives enough signals to trigger it to fire, a portion of the axon nearest the cell body depolarizes — the membrane potential quickly rises and then falls (in about 1,000th of a second).
S|This change triggers depolarization in the section of the axon next to it, and so on, until the rise and fall in charge has passed along the entire length of the axon.
S|After each section has fired, it enters a brief state of hyperpolarization, where its threshold is lowered, meaning it is less likely to be triggered again immediately.
S|Most often, it is (K+) and sodium (Na+) ions that generate the action potential.
S|Ions move in and out of the axons through voltage-gated ion channels and pumps.
S|**This is the process in brief:** 1.
S|Na+ channels open allowing Na+ to flood into the cell, making it more positive.
S|Once the cell reaches a certain charge, K+ channels open, allowing K+ to flow out of the cell.
S|Na+ channels then shut but K+ channels remain open allowing the positive charge to leave the cell.
S|The membrane potential plunges.
S|As the membrane potential returns to its resting state, the K+ channels shut.
S|Finally, the sodium/potassium pump transports Na+ out of the cell and K+ back into the cell ready for the next action potential.
S|Action potentials are described as “all or nothing” because they are always the same size.
S|The strength of a stimulus is transmitted using frequency.
S|For instance, if a stimulus is weak, the neuron will fire less often, and for a strong signal, it will fire more frequently.
S|### Myelin Most axons are covered by a white, waxy substance called myelin.
S|This coating insulates nerves and increases the speed at which impulses travel.
S|Myelin is created by Schwann cells in the peripheral nervous system and oligodendrocytes in the CNS.
S|There are small gaps in the myelin coating, called nodes of Ranvier.
S|The action potential jumps from gap to gap, allowing the signal to move much quicker.
S|Neurons are connected to each other and tissues so that they can communicate messages; however, they do not physically touch — there is always a gap between cells, called a synapse.
S|Synapses can be electrical or chemical.
S|In other words, the signal that is carried from the first nerve fiber (presynaptic neuron) to the next (postsynaptic neuron) is transmitted by an electrical signal or a chemical one.
S|### Chemical synapses Illustration of a synapse Image credit: U.S.
S|National Institutes of Health Once a signal reaches a synapse, it triggers the release of chemicals (neurotransmitters) into the gap between the two neurons; this gap is called the synaptic cleft.
S|The neurotransmitter diffuses across the synaptic cleft and interacts with receptors on the membrane of the postsynaptic neuron, triggering a response.
S|Chemical synapses are classified depending on the neurotransmitters they release: **Glutamergic** — releases glutamine.
S|They are often excitatory, meaning that they are more likely to trigger an action potential.
S|**GABAergic** — release GABA (gamma-Aminobutyric acid).
S|They are often inhibitory, meaning that they reduce the chance that the postsynaptic neuron will fire.
S|These are found between motor neurons and muscle fibers (the neuromuscular junction).
S|**Adrenergic** — release norepinephrine (adrenaline).
S|### Electrical synapses are less common but are found throughout the CNS.
S|Channels called gap junctions attach the presynaptic and postsynaptic membranes.
S|In gap junctions, the post- and presynaptic membranes are brought much closer together than in chemical synapses, meaning that they can pass electric current directly.
S|Electrical synapses work much faster than chemical synapses, so they are found in places where quick actions are necessary, for instance in defensive reflexes.
S|Chemical synapses can trigger complex reactions, but electrical synapses can only produce simple responses.
S|However, unlike chemical synapses, they are bidirectional — information can flow in either direction.
S|Neurons are one of the most fascinating types of cell in the human body.
S|They are essential for every action that our body and brain carry out.
S|It is the complexity of neuronal networks that gives us our personalities and our consciousness.
S|They are responsible for the most basic of actions, and the most intricate.
S|From automatic reflex actions to deep thoughts about the universe, neurons cover it all.

S|# How Brain Cells Work | Psychology Today Neurons communicate using electrical signals, or *action potentials*.
S|In order for a neuron to transmit its own signal, it must first be sufficiently activated by incoming signals from other neurons via its dendrites.
S|Then, an action potential is generated in its axon and carried along the length of the axon.
S|To travel from the axon of one neuron to the dendrite of another neuron, a signal must cross the gap between neurons (the synapse), where it is translated from an electrical signal to a chemical signal.
S|The action potential causes the release of molecules called *neurotransmitters*, which pass from one end of the synapse to the other.
S|Once these molecules cross the synapse, the chemical signal is translated back to an electrical signal that continues toward the next neuron.
S|The ultimate effect of a signal from one neuron on the next neuron depends on the function of the synapse, which is based on the type of neurotransmitter released.
S|*Excitatory synapses* send signals that encourage the creation of an action potential by the next neuron; *inhibitory synapses* work against it.
S|Each neuron receives signals from both excitatory and inhibitory synapses.
S|The interconnections between neurons allow for the formation of highly complex information-processing networks.
S|Over time, these connections can grow stronger or weaker depending on the neurons’ patterns of activation.
S|Changes in structure and function take place at the level of the synapses between neurons.
S|This capacity for change is called *synaptic plasticity* and it is essential to the brain’s ability to learn.
