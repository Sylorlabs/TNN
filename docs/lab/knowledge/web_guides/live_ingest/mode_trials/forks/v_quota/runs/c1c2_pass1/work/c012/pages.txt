S|# Curious Kids: what causes windy weather?
S|- Social Media Blog - Bureau of Meteorology > Curious Kids: what causes windy weather?
S|14 March 2018 *This is an article from, a series for children.
S|The Conversation is asking kids to send in questions they’d like an expert to answer.
S|All questions are welcome—serious, weird or wacky!
S|See the end of the article for how to ask a question.* **What causes windy weather?—Jake, aged 8, Melbourne.** Thank you for your great question, Jake.
S|Wind is just moving air, and air is a collection of different gases.
S|It’s mostly one type of gas, called nitrogen, but also lots of others, including oxygen – which we need to live.
S|When air is under pressure, it starts to move – and that causes wind.
S|I’ll explain what I mean by 'under pressure'.
S|Imagine you are blowing up a balloon.
S|As you blow more air into the balloon, the pressure builds inside.
S|If the pressure gets too great, the balloon could pop because the air has nowhere to move.
S|Just like the balloon, we don’t like to be under pressure, either.
S|Think of when your brother or sister or slightly annoying cousin gives you a great big bear hug.
S|You feel pressure because you’re getting squeezed.
S|Sometimes that can be nice but when the squeezing gets too much, the best way to get comfortable again is to break free and run.
S|It’s the same with air: when it’s under pressure, it tries to escape.
S|When the air inside a balloon is under pressure and you take your fingers off the neck part of it, the air rushes out—often with a bit of an embarrassing farty noise.
S|Well, that air rushing out is wind.
S|(And, let’s be honest, it’s why another name for a fart is 'breaking wind').
S|In the atmosphere, the same thing happens.
S|When pressure builds up in one place, the air rushes to another place where there is less pressure.
S|[Image 0: File 20180307 146691 1a8zxaz.jpg?ixlib=rb 1.1] **Illustration: The air doesn’t like to be under pressure just like us.
S|The wind is the result of the air trying to escape from high pressure.** **Credit: Mami Kempe/The Conversation, ** But what is causing this pressure in the atmosphere?
S|Well, as the sun heats up the surface of the earth, some areas get warmer and others stay cooler.
S|On the whole globe, for instance, the north and south poles are really cold.
S|This is because sunbeams pass over the top, so not much sunlight actually hits the ground.
S|Compare this to the equator, where temperatures are really warm, because the sunbeams are hitting it from directly above.
S|As you may know, warm air rises - just like when you see hot steam coming out the top of your kettle or a cooking pot at home.
S|[Image 1] **Illustration: When pressure builds up in one place, the air rushes to another place where there is less pressure.
S|Credit: Mami Kempe/The Conversation, ** My old teacher used to say 'You don’t get something for nothing, Andrew!' What he meant was that if air goes up in one place, it must come down in another place.
S|That other place will be where the air is not rising, and that’s normally where the cool areas are.
S|As this happens and the air comes down, it hits the ground and starts to build up.
S|When that air piles up too much, that pile of air will collapse and spread out, just like air rushing out of a balloon.
S|That air will rush towards the area that doesn’t have a big mound of air built up, and that will usually be a warm place where the air is rising.
S|You can feel this happening at the beach in summer, where the sun heats up the sand more than the water.
S|As heat builds up the air rises over the land and starts to fall over the ocean.
S|Soon there is more air over the ocean than the land, and a breeze starts as that air pile collapses.
S|So, put simply, wind is just air moving from one place where there is high pressure to another place where there is low pressure (a smaller pile of air).
S|Often, that’s from where it is cooler to where it is hotter.
S|And, thankfully, it rarely makes that farty sound.

S|# Energy - Student Resources: Wind Wind ### How does wind form?
S|Wind is **air in motion**.
S|Wind forms when the sun heats one part of the atmosphere differently than another part.
S|This causes expansion of warmer air, making less pressure where it is warm than where it is cooler.
S|Air always moves from high pressure to lower pressure, and this movement of air is wind.
S|During the day at a **coast**, the sun heats the air above the land more quickly than the air above water.
S|The warm air over the land expands and rises.
S|Cooler air from the ocean moves toward the land to take the place of the rising air, and makes a "sea breeze" that cools beachgoers in the summer.

S|# How do winds form?
S|– Eschooltoday ‘Wind’ is simply the flow of a huge amount of air, usually from a **high-pressure area** to a **low-pressure area.** ### How are winds formed?
S|Typically, this begins with the sun’s radiation, which is absorbed differently on the earth’s surface.
S|The earth’s surface is heated differently because of scenarios like cloud cover, mountains, valleys, water bodies, vegetation, and desert lands.
S|As a result of this uneven heating, the earth’s surfaces will vary a lot in temperature.
S|Air on surfaces with higher temperatures will then begin to rise because it is lighter (less dense).
S|As the air rises, it creates low atmospheric pressure.
S|Air on surfaces with cooler temperatures sinks (do not rise).
S|The sinking creates higher atmospheric pressure.
S|This behavior of warm gases or liquids moving upward and replaced by cooler particles is called **Convection**.
S|The energy moving during convection is called **convectional current.** ***Important***: *Hot air rises, and cool air sinks.
S|That brings about spatial differences in atmospheric pressure, caused by uneven heating.* Let us see this illustration below showing pressure and wind direction: How does the sun create winds?
S|In the diagram above, notice how cool air falls, resulting in high pressure, and moving towards regions of low pressure.
S|Anywhere and each time there are differences in atmospheric (air) pressure, there will be wind because air will move from the high-pressure area to the low-pressure area.
S|It also means that winds may be even stronger where the difference in the air pressure is greater.
S|A good example is how tropical depression forms, where warm air over hot tropical waters rises, and high-pressure cold air quickly rushes to fill the space.
