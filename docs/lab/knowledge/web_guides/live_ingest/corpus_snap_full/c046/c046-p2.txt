TITLE: From Past to Future: Understanding the Main Sources of Weather Data - meteoblue
**Was last summer unusually dry? Will tomorrow’s winds be strong enough to affect outdoor work? What temperatures should a building be designed to withstand? 
Each question calls for weather information, but answering them requires different kinds of data.**

[Image 0]

Weather data gains meaning from how it was produced and the period it describes. A local measurement captures conditions at a particular point, while a model 
can estimate weather where instruments are absent or calculate how conditions may develop.

For agriculture, renewable energy and infrastructure planning, these distinctions have practical consequences. The most suitable source depends on whether the 
task is to investigate an event, anticipate changing conditions or understand long-term patterns.

Four broad categories provide a useful starting point: observations record what instruments measure or detect; forecasts describe possible future weather; 
reanalyses combine observations and models to reconstruct the past; and climatologies summarise conditions over many years.

### **Observations: measuring the atmosphere in real time**

Weather observation begins with instruments. Surface stations measure temperature, pressure, precipitation and wind. Weather balloons sample the atmosphere 
vertically, while ships and buoys extend coverage across the oceans. Radar and satellites provide information over larger areas.

These systems observe weather in different ways. A thermometer measures temperature at a particular place and height. Radar uses reflected electromagnetic 
pulses to detect precipitation and its movement. Satellites measure radiation, from which information about clouds, temperature and moisture can be derived.

Together, these sources provide complementary views of the atmosphere. meteoblue uses local measurements alongside radar and 
satellite observations to build a fuller picture of current weather and assess how well forecasts match actual conditions.

Even when these sources are combined, observations have limits. A station records conditions at a specific location, which may differ from those on a nearby 
mountain slope or coastline. Observing networks are also unevenly distributed, and records may contain gaps or be affected by changes in instruments.

Radar and satellites extend coverage, but some weather variables are derived indirectly from the signals they detect. The accuracy of these estimates depends on
 the instrument and atmospheric conditions. Combining these observations helps fill gaps, although some local weather features may still go undetected.

### **Forecasts: moving the atmosphere forward in time**

To determine how the weather may develop, meteorologists rely on numerical models. These models represent the atmosphere through physical equations, 
calculating changes in air movement, temperature and moisture over the coming hours and days.

First, the model needs an estimate of the atmosphere’s current state. A process called data assimilation combines observations with model information to produce
 an analysis, including estimates for places without direct measurements. The model then advances this starting state forward in time.

Forecasts contain unavoidable uncertainty. Small errors in the initial conditions can grow, while models differ in how they represent terrain, clouds and other 
processes. Confidence generally decreases as the forecast extends further into the future.

To account for this uncertainty, ensemble forecasts calculate a range of possible outcomes. Multi-model 
approaches offer another perspective by comparing results from different forecasting systems. In practice, meteoblue combines numerical model information 
with measurements and post-processing methods to improve forecasts for individual locations.

Forecasts are appropriate for questions about the future: when might rain begin, or how strong could tomorrow’s wind become? They are not records of what 
eventually happened. Once the forecast period has passed, observations help assess their accuracy.

### **Reanalysis: rebuilding weather from the past**

Suppose we want hourly weather information for a remote location in 1985. There may have been no station there, or its records may be incomplete. Even existing 
measurements describe individual points rather than the whole atmosphere.

Reanalysis reconstructs past weather from historical observations and model calculations, including estimates for places where no measurements were taken. The 
same modelling system is applied throughout the period, making it easier to compare conditions across different years.

ECMWF ERA5 reanalysis, produced for the Copernicus Climate Change Service (C3S), provides hourly 
information from 1940 onwards. It covers a wide range of atmospheric and surface variables.

A reanalysis is not a measurement archive: many values are model-based estimates constrained by available observations. Nor is it simply a collection of old 
forecasts. It can incorporate observations that arrived after the original forecasts were issued and apply a consistent modelling system across decades.

This consistency makes reanalysis valuable for studying long-term variations and supporting agriculture, renewable energy and infrastructure assessments. 
Nevertheless, its quality depends on the historical observing network and model resolution. Small thunderstorms, local gusts and fine-scale terrain effects may 
be poorly represented.

For example, meteoblue provides reanalysis information alongside other historical simulation datasets, each with strengths and 
limitations linked to how it was produced.

### **Climatology: defining what is typical**

Historical weather data describes particular periods or events. Climatology asks what conditions are typical for a location and time of year.

A temperature of 30°C may be routine in one place and exceptional in another. Its significance becomes clearer when compared with a long record of local 
conditions.

Climatologies summarise such records statistically. They can describe average temperatures, rainfall, wind distributions or the frequency of frost and hot days.
 They are calculated from observations or model-based datasets, rather than representing a separate method for estimating weather at an individual moment.

The reference period matters. The World Meteorological Organization (WMO) defines climatological standard normals using 30-year periods, with 
1991–2020 as the most recent standard period. These are updated every decade. For long-term climate-change assessments, WMO also retains 1961–1990 as a fixed 
reference.

“This month was two degrees warmer than normal” therefore depends on the chosen baseline. In climatology, “normal” has a defined statistical meaning.

Historical baselines also provide a reference for climate projections, which estimate how conditions could change in the coming decades. 
Scientists produce these projections with climate models, running them under different assumptions about future greenhouse-gas emissions. Scenarios with higher 
or lower emissions allow them to examine several possible futures.

A projection might show, for example, how much warmer a region’s summers could become compared with 1991–2020. Other results describe changes in rainfall or the
 frequency of extreme heat. Comparing these projections with a historical baseline helps explain how future conditions could differ from what we currently 
consider normal.

### **The same location, four different questions**

Consider a vineyard. A sensor reading of 18.4°C at 08:00 is an observation. An expected temperature of 20°C tomorrow afternoon is a forecast. An hourly 
temperature series reconstructed for summer 1995 may come from reanalysis. The typical September maximum temperature is climatological information.

All four use degrees Celsius, but each answers a different question. A reanalysis value should not be treated as proof that a thermometer recorded that 
temperature at the exact location. Likewise, a climatological average cannot tell us what will happen on a particular afternoon.

The source also determines the detail users can expect. A station provides local information; a gridded dataset offers wider coverage while simplifying 
conditions within each cell; a forecast describes the future with uncertainty. The best choice depends on what you need to know and how much detail the task 
requires.

### **Connected sources, different purposes**

In practice, these categories work together. Observations help establish the current atmospheric state and verify forecasts. Historical observations support 
reanalysis, while long records from observations or simulations provide the basis for climatologies.

For those analysing past weather, the meteoblue Dataset API provides access to archived weather datasets, with options to select the
 variables, time period and geographic area needed for a particular study.

Knowing how weather data was produced helps explain why datasets differ and which source best answers a particular question. A station reading, forecast, 
reanalysis value and climate normal can all be useful – as long as each is interpreted in context.
