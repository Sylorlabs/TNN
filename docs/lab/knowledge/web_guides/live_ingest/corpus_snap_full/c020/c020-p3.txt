# What on Earth Are Prime Numbers? A


# Parent’s Quick Guide


##### Understanding a key primary maths idea — and helping it stick at home


If your child has come home talking about prime numbers and you’re not entirely sure what they are, you’re in excellent company. Here’s a quick refresher, plus 
some ways to make the idea stick.

A prime number is a whole number greater than 1 that can only be divided by 1 and itself. So 7 is prime, because the only whole numbers that divide into it 
neatly are 1 and 7. But 6 isn’t prime — it can be divided by 2 and by 3 as well as by 1 and 6.The first few primes are 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19 and 23. A 
couple of useful facts: 2 is the only even prime number (every other even number can be divided by 2). And 1 is not a prime — mathematicians decided long ago 
that it doesn’t count.

Why does any of this matter? Primes are the building blocks of every other number.

Every whole number can be made by multiplying primes together — 12 is 2 × 2 × 3, for example. This is the foundation for later work on factors, multiples and 
fractions.

To help it sink in at home, try a “divisibility test”. Pick a number — say 15 — and ask your child to find every number that divides into it without a 
remainder. If only 1 and the number itself work, it’s prime. Otherwise, it isn’t.

You can also play “prime or not?” in the car. Call out a number under 30 and see who answers first. Keep a list of primes on the fridge and add to it.

\[

A small warning: don’t worry if your child confuses primes with odd numbers. It’s a common mixup, because the early primes (3,5,7) are all odd. Reminding them 
about_9 (odd but not prime,because_3 \times 3 = 9) usually clears it up.
