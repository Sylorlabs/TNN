## How to Read Roman Numerals: The 3 Core Rules

Reading Roman numerals is a lot like reading words. You scan from left to right, and the position of each symbol changes the total value. There are really only 
three rules you need to know.

1. **The Additive Rule:** When you see a symbol of equal or lesser value placed *after* a symbol of greater value, you add them together. This is the most 
common rule. For example: VI is 5 + 1 = 6. And LXX is 50 + 10 + 10 = 70. Simple enough!

2. **The Subtractive Rule:** This is the one that trips most people up, but it’s easy once you see the pattern. When a smaller value symbol (only I, X, or C) is
 placed *before* a larger value symbol, you subtract the smaller from the larger. For example: IV is 5 – 1 = 4. XC is 100 – 10 = 90.

3. **The Repetition Rule:** You can repeat the symbols I, X, C, and M up to three times in a row to multiply their value. **Important: The symbols V, L, and D 
are never repeated.** For example: III is 1 + 1 + 1 = 3. CCC is 100 + 100 + 100 = 300. You would never write VV for 10; you just use X.

Let’s break down that library date, MCMIV, using these rules. We read left to right: M (1000), then CM (100 before 1000, so 1000 – 100 = 900), then IV (1 before
 5, so 5 – 1 = 4). Add them up: 1000 + 900 + 4 = 1904. See? It’s like solving a tiny, satisfying puzzle.

## Secrets to Mastering Roman Numerals

After years of working with historical texts and designs, I’ve picked up a few tricks that make reading Roman numerals second nature. Here are some of my go-to 
tips that I wish someone had told me from the start.

- **Break Down Large Numbers:** Don’t try to decipher a number like MCMLXXXVIII all at once. Chop it into manageable pieces based on the rules: M (1000) + CM 
(900) + LXXX (80) + VIII (8). Then just add the pieces: 1000 + 900 + 80 + 8 = 1988. It’s far less intimidating that way.

- **My “IC” Mistake:** When I was first learning, I tried to write 99 as IC (100 – 1). It seems logical, right? But it’s wrong. **The rule is you can only 
subtract a power of ten (I, X, or C) from the next two highest values.** So ‘I’ can only be put before V and X. ‘X’ only before L and C. ‘C’ only before D and 
M. The correct way to write 99 is XCIX (90 + 9). Don’t make my mistake!

- **Use a Mnemonic:** To remember the symbols in order from largest to smallest, try this silly sentence: **M**y **D**ear **C**at **L**oves **X**tra 
**V**itamins **I**ntensely. It works surprisingly well.

- **Handling Huge Numbers (The Vinculum):** Ever wonder how Romans wrote a million? They used a bar over the top, called a vinculum, which multiplies the value 
by 1,000. So, an X with a bar over it is 10,000. An M with a bar over it is 1,000,000. It’s a neat piece of trivia and shows up in some older texts.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why is there no zero in Roman numerals?

The concept of zero as a placeholder number wasn’t part of the Roman counting system. They simply didn’t need it for their calculations, so a symbol for it was 
never created.

### Can any Roman numeral be subtracted from another?

No, the rules are specific. You can only subtract I from V and X; X from L and C; and C from D and M. You can’t, for example, write VL for 45; it must be XLV.

### What’s the largest number you can write in Roman numerals?

Using the standard symbols, the largest number commonly written is 3,999 (MMMCMXCIX). By using the vinculum (a bar on top), you can create much larger numbers, 
but they are not typically seen today.

### Where are Roman numerals still used today?

You can find them all over! They’re used for Super Bowl numbering (like Super Bowl LIX), on clock faces, in movie and TV show copyrights, for monarchs and popes
 (like Queen Elizabeth II), and in outlines and book chapter headings.
