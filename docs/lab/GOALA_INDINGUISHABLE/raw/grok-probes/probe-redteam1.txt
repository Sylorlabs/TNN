## 1. Noise Is Not the Same as Human Imperfection

The study risks treating randomness as evidence of humanity. Jitter, grain, asymmetry, timing errors, and brush variation can all be produced by a random-number generator. Their presence does not prove that a person made a choice.

Human imperfection usually has structure. It is shaped by a body, a tool, a goal, a material, and a sequence of decisions. A hand-drawn line may wobble more on a difficult curve, slow down near a boundary, or repeat a personal habit. That is different from adding equal random jitter everywhere.

A useful distinction is:

- Noise is uncorrelated variation.
- Human imperfection is constrained, contextual variation.
- Expression is variation that changes how the work is read.

For example, randomizing the spacing of every note in a melody may sound sloppy. A performer who delays only certain notes before a phrase ending may sound expressive. Adding grain uniformly to a digital image looks like noise. Preserving more texture in areas that would plausibly receive wear, pressure, or repeated editing can suggest process.

Even this distinction is not reliable. Viewers can learn that “random irregularity” is a style convention and interpret it as intentional. A rough-looking mark may be read as human simply because contemporary visual culture has taught audiences to associate roughness with authenticity. The study should therefore test whether viewers detect human agency, not merely whether they detect irregularity.

## 2. The Model May Be Confusing Humanity with Traditional Skill

Many of the proposed signals are really signs of traditional production: brush drag, pigment buildup, canvas grain, camera imperfections, hand timing, and physical tool resistance. These may communicate “made by a person,” but they can also communicate “made with a familiar physical medium.”

That is not the same thing as human-likeness. A highly skilled traditional painting can feel emotionally empty, while a perfectly clean digital work can feel unmistakably authored.

The model is especially vulnerable with abstract digital art. Consider a work built from:

- Clean vector geometry
- Generative recursion
- Precise typography
- Shader-based color fields
- Glitch systems
- Algorithmic symmetry
- Interactive rules
- Synthetic sound or spatial audio

There may be no brush, canvas, camera, or physically plausible material to imitate. Adding simulated grain or hand wobble may not humanize such a work. It may merely make it look like a digital imitation of analog art.

Abstract digital art can feel human through other features:

- A distinctive rule system
- A surprising but legible transformation
- A strong relation between constraint and variation
- Conceptual specificity
- Timing that creates expectation and release
- A coherent history of revision
- A tension between what the system permits and what the artist selects

A perfectly regular generative pattern may feel more human than a noisy one if the regularity expresses a clear idea. Conversely, a textured and irregular image may feel impersonal if its choices are generic.

The study may therefore be measuring familiarity with human-made media rather than human-likeness itself.

## 3. When Human-Likeness Becomes an Obvious Fake

Deliberately added cues can become uncanny because they are too visible, too uniform, or unrelated to the work’s actual logic.

Examples include:

- A clean geometric composition with identical “hand-drawn” wobble applied to every edge. This reads as a filter, not as a hand.
- A digital painting with uniform paper grain placed over the entire image, including areas where grain should be hidden by dense paint or deep shadow.
- A song in which every note is randomly mistimed by the same amount. The result sounds mechanically humanized.
- A vocal track with artificial breaths inserted at regular intervals, making the performer’s body feel simulated.
- Animation with the same overshoot on every object, regardless of weight, force, or emotional context. It reads as a preset.
- Deliberate asymmetry added to a centered abstract composition without any change in visual balance. The irregularity looks decorative rather than intentional.
- A texture generator that repeats a small number of “brush marks.” Viewers notice the stamp pattern and infer automation.
- A supposedly expressive color palette in which every region receives the same saturation variation. The result looks like a global Instagram effect.
- Artificially uneven spacing in typography where the errors do not follow reading pressure, letter shape, or compositional purpose.
- Randomized detail added to a minimal work that originally depended on silence, emptiness, or precision. The added “humanity” destroys the work’s premise.
- A glitch effect used in an artwork with no technological, conceptual, or temporal reason for glitching. It becomes visual cosplay.

The common problem is not simply overuse. It is a mismatch between cue and context. Human actions have causes. If the claimed imperfection does not appear to result from a plausible action, process, or decision, it exposes itself as a surface effect.

There is also an important asymmetry: viewers often forgive real irregularity but distrust advertised irregularity. Once the audience notices that a work is trying to look human, the cues may become evidence of artificiality.

## 4. The Strongest Argument Against Human-Likeness as the Goal

The strongest argument is that human-likeness is a poor artistic objective because it rewards imitation of familiar imperfections rather than meaningful authorship.

If artists or models optimize for “feeling human,” they may converge on the same package of signals: slight asymmetry, warm colors, visible texture, irregular timing, rough edges, and controlled mess. This creates a new stereotype of humanity. The result can be less diverse, less surprising, and less alive than work that openly embraces artificiality.

Human beings do not always seek to appear human. They seek to communicate, discover, provoke, clarify, distort, entertain, or make something that did not previously exist. Some of the most powerful human works are austere, repetitive, mechanical, alien, precise, or deliberately impersonal.

The better goal is not human-likeness but meaningful agency and artistic consequence. A work should have choices that matter, a coherent relation between form and purpose, and effects that cannot be reduced to generic decoration. For generated art, the key questions should be:

- Does the work establish a specific logic?
- Do its variations serve that logic?
- Does it produce an interesting experience?
- Does it make the viewer revise expectations?
- Does it reveal or construct a point of view?
- Are its materials and processes relevant rather than merely simulated?

Human-likeness can remain one possible effect, but it should not be the standard of success. The strongest replacement is purposeful distinctiveness: work that feels made, not necessarily because it imitates human mistakes, but because its choices create a particular and consequential experience.
