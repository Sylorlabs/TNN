**1. What Dies, What Survives**

Many obvious human-likeness cues die in pure abstraction.

These cues mostly disappear:

- Faces, bodies, gestures, and gaze.
- Recognizable objects and places.
- Speech rhythm, accent, breath, and vocal imperfection.
- Instrument-specific noise, room acoustics, and physical performance traces.
- Cultural symbols, references, and recognizable artistic styles.
- Narrative events involving people or objects.
- Evidence that a human hand, body, or physical tool produced the result.

What survives are cues about decisions rather than subject matter.

The strongest surviving cues are:

- Persistent motifs that return in altered forms.
- Uneven repetition. Something recurs, but never mechanically.
- Selective imperfection. Some regions are unstable while others remain controlled.
- Hierarchy. The work has a foreground, background, buildup, release, and aftermath.
- Delayed resolution. Expectations are established and then complicated.
- Meaningful constraint. The piece seems to obey a particular rule chosen for this work.
- Memory. Later events appear to remember earlier events.
- Commitment. The work stays with an unusual color, interval, texture, or motion long enough for it to become significant.
- Irreversible development. The piece does not merely loop or wander; it changes what is possible later.
- Coherent inconsistency. The deviations feel related to the work's internal character rather than independently randomized.

In abstract media, human-likeness moves from “I recognize a human thing” to “I infer that someone made a sequence of choices.”

That inference is fragile. It can be produced by a strong internal structure, but it can also be falsely triggered by noise, novelty, or familiar artistic conventions.

**2. Imagination Versus Screensaver**

There is a real difference, but it is not simply beauty, complexity, or imperfection.

A screensaver is optimized to remain continuously pleasing. It avoids commitment. It can keep producing attractive events without making any event necessary.

An authored piece usually creates a local contract with the viewer. It introduces a pattern, teaches the viewer how to hear or see it, and then develops, breaks, or reinterprets that pattern.

A mesmerizing but clearly algorithmic fractal zoom often has:

- Continuous novelty without memory.
- Complexity at every scale but no meaningful hierarchy.
- Smooth transformation without decisive choices.
- Repetition caused by the algorithm rather than recurrence chosen by an author.
- No sense that one moment rules out another.
- No awkward pause, overextension, interruption, or withholding.
- A stable optimization target: keep looking, keep listening.

An authored abstract piece may instead contain a stubborn limitation. A color may return too late. A melody may repeat with one note missing. A shape may spend too long becoming something and then refuse to resolve. A texture may disappear when it seems most useful. These choices create the impression that the work has a point of view.

The key distinction is not randomness versus regularity. It is whether variation appears subordinate to a remembered intention.

A screensaver produces events. An authored work produces consequences.

**3. Hardest Stress Test**

Construct a 240x240 color animation with rich grain, slight frame jitter, nonuniform easing, imperfect symmetry, and carefully varied motion timing. Give it a recurring palette. Let several abstract shapes return in transformed versions. Add audio with expressive timing variation, changing texture density, small pitch deviations, and occasional pauses. Make every parameter look plausibly handmade.

It can score highly on all obvious human-likeness features:

- Jitter.
- Grain.
- Timing variation.
- Easing.
- Asymmetry.
- Imperfect repetition.
- Cross-modal coordination.
- Local unpredictability.
- Apparent physicality.

Yet it can still read as obviously AI.

The missing quality is selective necessity.

Every detail can look plausible while no detail feels chosen for a reason. The jitter is distributed everywhere. The grain has no privileged relationship to the form. Timing varies, but not in response to an emerging tension. Motifs return, but their returns do not alter the meaning of what came before. The work contains many signs of human behavior without having a particular problem that it is trying to solve.

This piece would imitate the surface statistics of authorship while lacking authorship's uneven allocation of attention.

A human may obsess over one transition and leave another blunt. They may preserve an odd mistake because it becomes structurally useful. They may abandon an attractive development because it violates the work's direction. The stress-test piece has no such sacrifice. It is too evenly competent.

Its deeper failure is the absence of an implied private constraint: a rule, memory, obsession, refusal, or unresolved tension that explains why this work had to become this particular work.

**4. The Three Features I Would Keep**

**First: Persistent, transformed memory**

A motif should return across color, sound, and motion, but never as a copy. The later appearance should prove that the work remembers the earlier one.

This is stronger than repetition. It creates identity and continuity.

**Second: Uneven intentional constraint**

The work should have a narrow rule or obsession that shapes many decisions, while allowing some consequences to become surprising. Examples include refusing one expected color transition, repeatedly approaching but avoiding a cadence, or making every shape inherit one small defect from an earlier state.

This separates a coherent piece from a general-purpose generator.

**Third: Structured consequence**

Events should change the future. A rupture should remove an option. A pause should create pressure. A new texture should contaminate later textures. A visual transformation should make the next transformation feel earned or difficult.

This produces the sense of an unfolding thought rather than a stream of attractive samples.

Together, these features create the strongest evidence of imagination in abstraction: memory, commitment, and consequence. Jitter, grain, and timing variation are useful only when they serve those deeper structures. Otherwise they are cosmetic evidence of humanity, and a capable generator can reproduce them easily.
