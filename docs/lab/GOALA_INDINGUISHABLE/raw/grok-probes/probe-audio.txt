MICRO-TIMING DEVIATIONS

What it is: Notes begin slightly before or after their nominal grid position.

Why a human listener detects it: Perfectly aligned attacks feel mechanically sequenced, especially in repeated rhythms. Small timing differences create the impression of physical performance.

Deterministic implementation: For note index `i`, compute a signed hash value in `[-1, 1]`, then map it to a timing offset such as `±8 ms`. Reduce the offset for important phrase peaks, or use a smooth phrase-dependent bias so an entire phrase leans slightly forward or backward. Keep repeated motifs consistent enough to sound intentional.


VELOCITY AND DYNAMICS SHAPING

What it is: Note loudness changes according to melody, rhythm, harmony, and phrase importance.

Why a human listener detects it: Human performers naturally emphasize arrivals, peaks, downbeats, syncopations, and emotionally important notes. Constant velocity makes even a good melody sound machine-made.

Deterministic implementation: Calculate velocity from several terms:

- Phrase envelope: louder near the phrase climax.
- Melodic contour: slightly louder on ascending or high-register notes.
- Metrical position: moderate emphasis on strong beats.
- Note role: melody louder than accompaniment.
- Deterministic variation: a small hash-based offset, such as `±3` MIDI velocity units.

Smooth the result across neighboring notes so it behaves like a performance rather than independent random values.


PHRASING ARCS

What it is: A musical phrase gradually grows, reaches a local high point, and relaxes over four to eight bars.

Why a human listener detects it: Humans organize notes into gestures rather than treating every note as an equally important event. Phrase-level direction is one of the clearest signals of intentional performance.

Deterministic implementation: Define a phrase length in beats or bars. Use a smooth asymmetric envelope, such as a sine curve or cubic Bézier curve, with the peak around 60 to 75 percent of the phrase. Apply it to velocity, brightness, note duration, vibrato depth, and accompaniment density. Reset or partially overlap the envelope at phrase boundaries.


INTENTIONAL DISSONANCE AND RESOLUTION

What it is: A controlled clash of pitches, followed by a musically meaningful movement into consonance.

Why a human listener detects it: Tension and release communicate expectation, direction, and emotional intention. Random dissonance sounds accidental, while prepared and resolved dissonance sounds composed.

Deterministic implementation: Select tension points from harmonic position, phrase position, or dominant-function chords. Add deterministic choices such as:

- A passing tone one semitone above a chord tone.
- A suspended fourth resolving downward.
- A chromatic approach note resolving by semitone.
- A brief upper extension held across a chord change.

Ensure the dissonant note is prepared by the preceding harmony and resolves within a specified number of beats. Make the resolution louder, longer, or brighter than the tension note when appropriate.


TIMBRAL EVOLUTION WITHIN A SINGLE NOTE

What it is: The spectral character of a note changes during its attack, sustain, and decay.

Why a human listener detects it: Acoustic instruments do not produce a static waveform. Their brightness, noise content, harmonics, and resonance evolve continuously after the key is played.

Deterministic implementation: Use a time-varying filter and harmonic envelope. For example:

- Start with a short bright attack.
- Move the low-pass cutoff downward during the first 100 to 300 ms.
- Add a slow, small cutoff rise near phrase peaks.
- Let higher harmonics decay faster than the fundamental.
- Add a deterministic amplitude-modulated noise layer during the attack.
- Vary the envelope according to note velocity and register.

The changes should be correlated with note role and dynamics rather than independently randomized.


MISTAKES THAT SERVE EXPRESSION

What it is: A small, controlled imperfection that reinforces the musical gesture.

Why a human listener detects it: Human performance includes physical limitations, anticipation, hesitation, and expressive risk. Listeners accept an imperfection when it has a clear musical cause.

Deterministic implementation: Define a very low-rate event rule, such as one eligible expressive deviation every 32 to 64 notes. Choose only notes that satisfy musical conditions, such as:

- A phrase-ending note arriving 5 to 15 ms late.
- A repeated note receiving a slightly weaker attack.
- A grace note being nearly masked by the main note.
- A note near a leap being slightly delayed.
- A held note beginning with a brief pitch or amplitude instability.

Use fixed content-based selection rather than random selection. Never apply the effect to arbitrary notes, and avoid repeated use of the same mistake pattern.


RUBATO

What it is: Local tempo flexing that stretches or compresses time around musical tension.

Why a human listener detects it: Performers often delay arrivals, accelerate through transitions, or relax at phrase endings. Constant tempo can make expressive music feel mechanically quantized.

Deterministic implementation: Build a tempo multiplier from phrase and harmony state. For example:

- Gradually accelerate toward a cadence.
- Slightly slow before an important arrival.
- Delay a phrase peak by a few milliseconds.
- Recover the time after the arrival so the long-term bar position remains stable.
- Use a smooth spline or integrated tempo curve so there are no discontinuities.

Keep rubato at a small scale, commonly within about 1 to 4 percent of local tempo, with larger changes reserved for explicit transitions.


HUMAN BREATH, BODY SOUNDS, AND ORGANIC BYPRODUCTS

What it is: Quiet, musically placed non-tonal sounds associated with physical performance.

Why a human listener detects it: Breath, finger contact, bow noise, key noise, and small resonances imply a sound-producing body. They can make an otherwise synthetic instrument feel physically performed.

Deterministic implementation: Add very quiet filtered noise or short synthesized transients tied to note events:

- A breath-like filtered noise swell before a phrase entrance.
- A short key or finger transient at selected attacks.
- A faint release noise when a note ends.
- A subtle valve, bow, or pick-like noise whose level depends on velocity.
- A breath noise layer that follows phrase amplitude and is reduced during dense passages.

Use deterministic hash selection and phrase rules. Keep these sounds below the musical signal, with event density low enough that they do not become a repeating sample pattern.


MOTIVIC DEVELOPMENT

What it is: A recognizable short idea returns in altered forms across the piece.

Why a human listener detects it: Coherent transformation signals memory, intention, and composition. Random variation can imitate complexity but usually does not create a sense of authorship.

Deterministic implementation: Define a seed motif with intervals and rhythmic values, then transform it by musical rules:

- Transpose it to the current chord.
- Invert or reverse its interval contour.
- Expand or compress its rhythm.
- Move it to another register.
- Remove one note or insert a passing tone.
- Change its articulation or timbre.
- Present a fragment during a transition and the complete motif at a climax.

Use the same transformations at structurally meaningful locations, such as phrase endings, section starts, and the final cadence.


ARTICULATION AND NOTE-TO-NOTE INTERACTION

What it is: Notes connect, separate, overlap, or accent one another according to instrument and musical role.

Why a human listener detects it: Human players shape the boundary between notes. Uniform gaps or identical overlaps reveal a sequencer even when timing and velocity are varied.

Deterministic implementation: Select articulation from note context:

- Use legato overlap for stepwise melodic motion.
- Use shorter releases after leaps or repeated notes.
- Add accents after rests.
- Shorten accompaniment notes while sustaining melody notes.
- Increase overlap on phrase interiors and reduce it near phrase boundaries.
- Vary release time according to velocity and register.

For a melody, calculate the next interval, rhythmic position, phrase location, and harmony before choosing the envelope. This is often more perceptually important than adding more noise or randomness.


RANKING

1. Phrasing arcs and musical dynamics  
   Listeners quickly notice when music has no direction or emotional hierarchy.

2. Motivic development  
   Coherent repetition and transformation strongly distinguish composition from note generation.

3. Micro-timing and articulation  
   Perfect timing, identical note gaps, and uniform attacks create an immediate sequenced impression.

4. Timbral evolution within notes  
   Static synthesized tones are recognizable, particularly when used for exposed melodies.

5. Rubato  
   It is highly effective in expressive solo or sparse music, but less noticeable in dense rhythmic music.

6. Intentional dissonance and resolution  
   This strongly affects perceived musical intelligence, although listeners may describe it as composition rather than performance.

7. Velocity shaping  
   It is important, but it overlaps with phrasing arcs and can be less obvious when the timbre already has strong amplitude movement.

8. Motivated expressive imperfections  
   A good one can be memorable, but its absence is usually less noticeable than the absence of basic dynamics or timing variation.

9. Organic byproducts  
   These can increase realism, especially for instrument-like synthesis, but they cannot compensate for poor musical phrasing.

10. Additional articulation and note-to-note interaction  
   This can rank higher than organic byproducts in exposed solo parts and lower in dense arrangements. It is especially important for making a part sound physically played.


BACKFIRE RISKS

MICRO-TIMING

Risk: Independent hash-based offsets can sound like jitter or poor synchronization.

Mitigation: Use correlated timing curves, constrain the range by tempo and note duration, and keep important arrivals relatively stable. Do not let every instrument deviate in unrelated directions.


DYNAMICS

Risk: If every high note is louder, the result sounds like a formula rather than an expressive performer. Excessive velocity variation can also make the melody sound uneven.

Mitigation: Combine pitch contour with phrase position, harmonic function, rhythmic role, and local repetition. Apply a smooth envelope and limit per-note changes.


PHRASING ARCS

Risk: A perfectly repeated four-bar volume swell reveals the algorithm.

Mitigation: Vary phrase length within a musical range, shift the climax position, and let some phrases build through brightness or density instead of loudness alone. Section-level structure should control the arcs.


RUBATO

Risk: Unmotivated tempo changes sound like unstable playback or a failing clock.

Mitigation: Tie rubato to cadences, transitions, phrase peaks, and harmonic tension. Integrate the tempo curve so the timing remains coherent and avoids abrupt corrections.


INTENTIONAL DISSONANCE

Risk: Dissonance without preparation or resolution sounds like wrong notes.

Mitigation: Use recognizable harmonic functions, limit the duration of clashes, and make the resolution perceptually clear. A dissonance should change the listener's expectation before it is resolved.


TIMBRAL EVOLUTION

Risk: Repeated filter sweeps produce an obvious synthesizer signature. Excessive brightness movement can sound like an automated effect.

Mitigation: Use different time constants for different note roles, scale the movement by velocity and register, and avoid applying the same envelope to every note. The timbral change should support phrasing rather than call attention to itself.


EXPRESSIVE MISTAKES

Risk: Too many mistakes, or mistakes placed on structurally unimportant notes, sound sloppy or deliberately randomized.

Mitigation: Use them rarely and only where a human performer might plausibly struggle or hesitate. Prefer timing, attack, or articulation imperfections over wrong pitches unless the harmonic context makes the wrong pitch clearly intentional.


ORGANIC BYPRODUCTS

Risk: Repeated noise bursts become a sample-library loop or a conspicuous artificial layer.

Mitigation: Make them quiet, sparse, spectrally varied, and dependent on phrase context. They should disappear when removed from the mix rather than become the main evidence of “human” performance.


MOTIVIC DEVELOPMENT

Risk: Repeating a motif too literally becomes predictable or mechanical.

Mitigation: Preserve the motif's interval contour or rhythmic identity while changing register, harmony, articulation, and density. Give it meaningful variation at section boundaries.


ARTICULATION

Risk: Rule-based legato and staccato can sound like a fixed decision tree.

Mitigation: Use continuous values rather than only two states. Let overlap, release, attack strength, and accent amount depend on interval size, phrase position, tempo, register, and the next note.


OVERALL PRIORITY

The strongest result usually comes from coordinated structure: a recognizable motif, phrase-level dynamics, context-aware articulation, small correlated timing deviations, and evolving timbre. Deterministic variation should be constrained by musical causes. Hash functions are useful for choosing among equivalent expressive options, but they should not be the source of the musical logic itself.
