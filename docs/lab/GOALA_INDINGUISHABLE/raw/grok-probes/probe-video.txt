## (1) EASING

What it is: Motion changes speed gradually instead of moving at a constant velocity.

Why a human viewer detects it: Natural gestures usually have preparation, acceleration, and settling. Constant-speed movement feels mechanical and procedural.

Deterministic implementation idea: For normalized progress `t = frame_index / segment_length`, use smoothstep:

`p = t * t * (3 - 2 * t)`

Map `p` between the start and end positions. Use stronger easing for large gestures and lighter easing for small movements.

## (2) ANTICIPATION AND OVERSHOOT

What it is: An object briefly moves away from its intended direction, then passes the target slightly before settling.

Why a human viewer detects it: Anticipation communicates intention. Overshoot suggests momentum, effort, and a body correcting itself.

Deterministic implementation idea: Divide the movement into phases:

- Frames 0 to 10 percent: move a small distance backward.
- Frames 10 to 85 percent: move toward the target using eased interpolation.
- Frames 85 to 100 percent: pass the target by a fixed amount, then return.

Set the anticipation and overshoot from object properties or a content hash, for example:

`overshoot = base_amount * (0.8 + 0.2 * hash01(object_index))`

The hash must remain fixed for the entire render.

## (3) SECONDARY MOTION

What it is: Parts of an object continue moving after the main body has started or stopped.

Why a human viewer detects it: Delayed motion resembles flexible materials, joints, hair, cloth, or layered drawing. It makes the object feel connected rather than rigid.

Deterministic implementation idea: Give each secondary part a delayed version of the main path:

`secondary_position(frame) = main_position(frame - delay_frames)`

Use a larger delay for flexible parts and a smaller delay for rigid parts. A trailing edge can also interpolate toward the main position:

`trail += stiffness * (main_position - trail)`

Use fixed stiffness values per object.

## (4) IRREGULAR RHYTHM

What it is: Repeated actions have slightly different durations or spacing instead of occurring at perfectly regular intervals.

Why a human viewer detects it: Human timing contains emphasis, hesitation, compression, and release. Perfectly repeating intervals are easy to recognize as machine-generated.

Deterministic implementation idea: Build a fixed timing sequence from a low-discrepancy or hash-derived pattern:

`durations = [18, 22, 19, 25, 20]`

Or derive each duration from an object-specific content hash within a narrow range:

`duration_i = base_duration + hash_signed(i) * timing_variation`

Keep variation restrained, such as plus or minus 10 percent. The sequence should be generated once, not independently each frame.

## (5) MOTIVATED CAMERA

What it is: The virtual camera moves because it is following an important object, event, or change in the composition.

Why a human viewer detects it: Viewers infer attention and intention from camera movement. A camera that responds to meaningful action feels directed; an arbitrary pan feels like an effect.

Deterministic implementation idea: Define an interest point from the active object or event:

`interest = weighted_average(object_centers, attention_weights)`

Move the camera toward that point with delayed easing:

`camera_position += camera_follow_rate * (interest - camera_position)`

Add camera shifts only at narrative events such as a reveal, collision, large color change, or directional reversal. Use a small lead in the direction of travel so the camera appears to anticipate the action.

## (6) SQUASH AND STRETCH

What it is: An object compresses along one axis and expands along another while keeping approximately constant visual volume.

Why a human viewer detects it: Deformation communicates weight, impact, acceleration, and elasticity. It makes an abstract shape behave as though it has substance.

Deterministic implementation idea: At an impact, define a normalized impact progress `u` from 0 to 1. Use a squash scale:

`scale_y = 1 - amount * sin(pi * u)`

Preserve area approximately with:

`scale_x = 1 / scale_y`

For stretching during fast movement, scale along the velocity direction and compensate across the perpendicular direction. Limit the scale range, such as 0.85 to 1.2, to avoid a cartoon loop that repeats too obviously.

## (7) HELD FRAMES AND HOLDS

What it is: Motion pauses briefly at a meaningful position before continuing.

Why a human viewer detects it: Pauses create attention, thought, emphasis, and readable poses. Continuous motion without rest often feels like a simulation running at a constant rate.

Deterministic implementation idea: Add fixed holds at structural points:

- Before a major movement: 2 to 4 frames.
- At a pose or reveal: 3 to 8 frames.
- After an impact: 1 to 3 frames.

Hold the object position while allowing a very small secondary motion, such as one pixel of settling or a slow color shift. This prevents the hold from looking like a frozen render error.

## (8) TEXTURE OF MOTION

What it is: A small, controlled deviation from a clean path that gives the motion a drawn or performed quality.

Why a human viewer detects it: Perfect paths expose mathematical construction. Small coherent imperfections suggest a hand, a tool, or a performer making continuous adjustments.

Deterministic implementation idea: Add a low-amplitude, low-frequency offset:

`offset = amplitude * sin(phase + frame_index * frequency)`

Use separate phases derived from a stable object hash. Avoid independent random-looking noise each frame. The wobble should be continuous, correlated across neighboring frames, and usually less than 1 to 3 percent of the object size.

## (9) NARRATIVE BEAT STRUCTURE

What it is: Motion is organized into recognizable units with an entrance, a development, a peak, and a resolution.

Why a human viewer detects it: Viewers naturally interpret grouped changes as gestures or events. Structured beats imply that the motion has an intention rather than being a continuous parameter sweep.

Deterministic implementation idea: Divide the animation into fixed gesture segments:

- Establish: the object appears or gathers energy.
- Initiate: it begins moving.
- Develop: the path or form changes.
- Peak: the strongest displacement, color change, or deformation occurs.
- Resolve: it settles, exits, or hands attention to another object.

Use frame ranges and deterministic state transitions. For example, a color field can swell toward an object, briefly peak when it reaches it, then recede while another shape becomes active.

## (10) HIERARCHY OF INTENT AND CAUSAL RESPONSE

What it is: Not every element moves equally; secondary changes visibly respond to a primary event.

Why a human viewer detects it: Human-authored animation usually has a reason for each important change. When one action causes another, the viewer perceives intention, even in abstract imagery.

Deterministic implementation idea: Create a fixed dependency graph between objects or layers. For example:

1. A primary shape turns toward a target.
2. The camera follows the turn.
3. A nearby color field reacts after a delay.
4. A trailing shape stretches during the turn.
5. The affected field settles after the primary shape stops.

Use event timestamps and fixed delays rather than independent oscillators. Let the strength of each response be determined by distance, object index, or a content hash. This gives the animation a readable chain of cause and effect.

## RANKING

1. Narrative beat structure  
2. Easing  
3. Motivated camera  
4. Anticipation and overshoot  
5. Hierarchy of intent and causal response  
6. Holds  
7. Secondary motion  
8. Irregular rhythm  
9. Squash and stretch  
10. Texture of motion  

Narrative structure and easing are usually the most detectable because they affect the overall readability of every gesture. A video can survive without squash and stretch if the objects are abstract, but it is difficult for it to feel authored if every movement has the same timing and no clear beginning or resolution.

Motivated camera movement ranks highly when the camera is prominent. If the animation is presented in a fixed frame with no camera motion, that feature becomes less important.

Texture of motion is valuable, but it should be subtle. Viewers may not identify it directly; they mainly notice when the underlying paths are excessively perfect.

## BACKFIRE RISKS

### Easing

Too much easing makes every object appear to accelerate and decelerate identically. This creates a recognizable software signature.

Use different easing strengths for different actions. A sharp turn, a heavy object, and a floating field should not share the same timing curve.

### Anticipation and Overshoot

If every movement winds up and overshoots, the animation can look like a spring system or a preset bounce effect.

Use anticipation only when the action benefits from preparation. Keep overshoot proportional to speed, object softness, or impact strength, and allow some movements to stop cleanly.

### Secondary Motion

If the delay is constant and visible on every part, the result can look like a mechanical lag filter.

Use different delays for different layers. Some parts should move immediately, while others should lag, settle, or remain still.

### Irregular Rhythm

Unstructured timing variation can look like jitter or faulty playback rather than human timing.

Change durations around meaningful beats, not at arbitrary frame positions. Keep the variation small and make important events easier to read than minor events.

### Motivated Camera

A camera that follows every moving object too closely can look like an automatic tracking algorithm. Excessive correction also creates artificial hovering.

Use camera holds, imperfect but stable framing, and explicit attention priorities. The camera should sometimes stay still while the subject moves through the frame.

### Squash and Stretch

Repeated uniform deformation can look like a bouncing logo or a physics demo.

Reserve deformation for acceleration, contact, impact, or strong directional changes. Vary the amount and recovery time according to the apparent material.

### Holds

Too many holds can make the animation feel like a slideshow. Holds that occur at equal intervals become another form of metronomic timing.

Place holds at expressive poses, reveals, and impacts. Vary their duration within a narrow, deliberate range.

### Texture of Motion

High-frequency wobble is easily recognized as noise, camera shake, or random-number output.

Use low-frequency, spatially coherent offsets. The deviation should support the movement direction and fade during holds or important poses.

### Narrative Beat Structure

Overly explicit beats can feel like a repeated template: enter, bounce, pause, exit.

Vary the length and emphasis of each phase. Some gestures can dissolve without a peak; others can interrupt an earlier motion or transfer attention to another object.

### Hierarchy of Intent and Causal Response

If every event triggers a visible chain reaction, the animation can feel like a rule-based demo.

Use selective causality. Some responses should be delayed, some partial, and some absent. The viewer should be able to infer relationships without seeing the same formula applied everywhere.
