## (1) Hand-Jitter / Wobbly Linework

**What it is:** Slight, uneven deviations in lines, contours, and repeated marks.

**Why a human detects it:** Human hands rarely produce perfectly uniform curves or identical spacing.

**Does it survive at 240x240?** Yes, if the variation is large enough to affect visible edges. Subpixel noise will disappear or become compression-like noise.

**Deterministic implementation idea:** Define each stroke by control points, then offset each point by a smooth deterministic hash-based value between -2 and +2 pixels. Interpolate the offsets so the line wobbles in broad movements rather than vibrating at every pixel.

## (2) Asymmetric but Deliberate Composition

**What it is:** Unequal placement of masses, empty space, and visual weight, balanced by intention rather than symmetry.

**Why a human detects it:** Human compositions often privilege one side, create tension, and avoid mechanical centering.

**Does it survive at 240x240?** Very strongly. Large-scale placement survives better than almost any texture detail.

**Deterministic implementation idea:** Place the dominant form around 35 percent from one edge, add a smaller counterweight near the opposite edge, and leave an irregular area of negative space. Use fixed coordinates perturbed by small deterministic offsets.

## (3) Pentimenti / Overpainting

**What it is:** Visible traces of earlier forms, erased shapes, revisions, or painted-over decisions.

**Why a human detects it:** Viewers interpret remnants as evidence that someone changed their mind while making the image.

**Does it survive at 240x240?** Yes, but only when the traces are broad and intentional. Fine underdrawing will vanish.

**Deterministic implementation idea:** Render an earlier composition first, partially cover it with opaque shapes, then leave selected fragments visible along edges and through translucent overpaint. Use a fixed mask generated from low-frequency coordinate noise, not pixel noise.

## (4) Brush Texture and Pressure Variation

**What it is:** Marks that vary in width, opacity, density, and edge softness as if made with changing physical pressure.

**Why a human detects it:** Real tools leave marks that accelerate, slow down, thicken, fade, and deposit pigment unevenly.

**Does it survive at 240x240?** Moderately. Width and opacity variation survive; tiny bristle details usually do not.

**Deterministic implementation idea:** Along each stroke, vary width from 1 to 5 pixels and opacity from 55 to 100 percent using a deterministic smooth function. Break the edge with a few larger hash-selected notches instead of adding uniform grain everywhere.

## (5) Focal Hierarchy

**What it is:** One region receives the strongest contrast, detail, color intensity, or shape complexity.

**Why a human detects it:** Human-made images usually guide attention rather than distributing visual importance equally.

**Does it survive at 240x240?** Extremely well. Focal hierarchy is primarily a large-scale relationship.

**Deterministic implementation idea:** Choose one focal zone and give it the highest local contrast and most specific edges. Reduce contrast, saturation, and mark density toward the corners using distance from the focal zone.

## (6) Deliberate Imbalance / Visual Rhyme Across the Canvas

**What it is:** Unequal forms are connected by repeated colors, gestures, angles, or rhythms.

**Why a human detects it:** Viewers recognize intentional relationships even when objects are not identical or symmetrically arranged.

**Does it survive at 240x240?** Yes, provided the repeated relationships operate at medium or large scale.

**Deterministic implementation idea:** Repeat a curved gesture three times at different sizes and positions. Keep one feature constant, such as a red accent or diagonal direction, while changing scale, opacity, and shape. Avoid exact duplication.

## (7) Cultural Markers

**What it is:** References to recognizable periods, symbols, visual traditions, jokes, or shared cultural imagery.

**Why a human detects it:** Cultural references imply an imagined context and a maker addressing an audience.

**Does it survive at 240x240?** Sometimes. Simple, iconic markers survive; subtle quotations and text-like details may become unreadable.

**Deterministic implementation idea:** Use one simplified, nonliteral marker, such as a deliberately distorted sunburst, propaganda-like stripe arrangement, pixel-era icon, or recognizable but altered symbol. Keep it large enough to occupy at least 15 to 25 pixels.

## (8) Material Honesty

**What it is:** Texture and accumulation that behave like a real material, such as canvas, paper, dry paint, or thick pigment.

**Why a human detects it:** Consistent interaction between marks and surface suggests a physical making process rather than arbitrary decoration.

**Does it survive at 240x240?** Moderately. Broad grain, edge buildup, and paint deposits survive; fine canvas weave does not.

**Deterministic implementation idea:** Create a low-contrast directional canvas pattern, then let strokes modify it: thick strokes obscure grain, dry strokes reveal it, and overlapping strokes increase opacity or brightness at their intersections.

## (9) Limited Purposeful Palette Versus Exhaustive Color

**What it is:** A restricted set of colors with a few intentional accents rather than every possible hue.

**Why a human detects it:** Artists commonly make color decisions within a chosen emotional or material world.

**Does it survive at 240x240?** Very strongly. Palette structure remains visible even when detail is lost.

**Deterministic implementation idea:** Start with four to seven base colors, including one dominant neutral or field color. Assign each shape a palette role, then reserve one high-chroma accent for the focal area. Generate mixtures only by deterministic interpolation between chosen colors.

## (10) Coherent Ambiguity and Motif Development

**What it is:** The image suggests a recurring subject, gesture, or idea without explaining it completely, while related forms evolve across the canvas.

**Why a human detects it:** Human imagination often produces images that are suggestive rather than perfectly explicit. Repeated motifs imply thought, memory, or association.

**Does it survive at 240x240?** Yes, if the motif is visible as a broad silhouette, color relationship, or repeated gesture. Tiny symbolic details will not survive.

**Deterministic implementation idea:** Choose one underlying motif, such as a bent branch, eye-like oval, rising staircase, or interrupted horizon. Repeat it three times with controlled changes in scale, orientation, and occlusion. Keep enough variation that it feels developed rather than stamped.

## RANKING

Ordered by whether a viewer would notice its absence most readily:

1. Focal hierarchy  
2. Asymmetric but deliberate composition  
3. Limited purposeful palette  
4. Coherent ambiguity and motif development  
5. Deliberate imbalance and visual rhyme  
6. Hand-jitter and wobbly linework  
7. Brush texture and pressure variation  
8. Pentimenti and overpainting  
9. Material honesty  
10. Cultural markers  

Large-scale organization ranks highest because it remains legible at 240x240. Texture and process evidence help, but they cannot rescue an image whose composition feels evenly distributed or mechanically assembled. Cultural markers can be powerful for some viewers, but they are less universally detectable and may be missed at low resolution.

## BACKFIRE RISKS

### Focal Hierarchy

If the focal point is too obvious, it can resemble a procedural saliency map. A perfectly centered bright spot or uniformly reduced detail toward the edges may look like a textbook composition rule. Make the focal area slightly off-center and allow one or two strong distractions.

### Asymmetric Composition

Randomly moving objects away from the center does not create intention. It can look like careless placement or an automated layout rule. Connect the unequal regions through shared color, direction, or rhythm.

### Limited Purposeful Palette

A palette that is too narrow can resemble a preset filter or color-generation constraint. Include small, controlled temperature shifts and one or two mixed transition colors so the palette feels chosen rather than quantized.

### Coherent Motif Development

Repeating the same motif at regular intervals or with identical geometry creates a stamp or tiling effect. Vary scale, interruption, opacity, and orientation while preserving only one or two recognizable relationships.

### Deliberate Imbalance and Visual Rhyme

Repeated accents can become a conspicuous algorithmic pattern, especially when they occur at equal distances or identical sizes. Use irregular spacing and let one expected repetition be missing or partially hidden.

### Hand-Jitter

Independent pixel-level randomness reads as digital noise, not a hand. Use smooth, low-frequency deviations along meaningful strokes, with larger variation at turns and endpoints than along straight sections.

### Brush Texture and Pressure Variation

Uniform texture applied everywhere looks like a filter. Tie texture to stroke direction, speed, overlap, and opacity. Some marks should be smooth, some dry, and some nearly solid.

### Pentimenti / Overpainting

Visible underlayers can look like a deliberately added “human artifact” if they are too frequent, too evenly distributed, or too clearly decorative. Show earlier decisions mainly near meaningful edges, intersections, and partially concealed forms.

### Material Honesty

Canvas grain and paint buildup can become procedural noise when they have no relationship to the marks. Let the surface react to the composition: thick regions should cover or compress the grain, while sparse regions should reveal it.

### Cultural Markers

A symbol that is too legible can make the image feel like a generated collage of references. A marker that is too obscure contributes nothing. Use one altered, partially ambiguous reference and integrate it with the image’s existing palette and geometry.
