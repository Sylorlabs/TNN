Let \(Y=1\) mean “the adversarial judgment is correct.” The existing judgment stream and confidence values should not be treated as resolution channels: they are observationally equivalent under the two hypotheses, and confidence is explicitly anti-informative. For a binary channel with balanced classes and accuracy \(p\), a useful rough estimate is:

\[
I(Y;R) \approx 1-H_2(p)
\]

where \(R\) is the resolution result. Thus, 50% accuracy gives 0 bits, 75% gives about 0.19 bits, 90% gives about 0.53 bits, and 95% gives about 0.71 bits. One binary resolution channel cannot provide more than 1 bit per case.

1. **Class-specific analytic causal checker**

   Implement a deterministic checker for each perturbation class that computes the task-relevant property directly from the stimulus representation and perturbation metadata. Examples:

   - `colordisc`: compare measured color difference against the task threshold in a calibrated color space.
   - `colorconst`: verify that the intended color relation remains invariant under the transformation.
   - `shapetrans`: calculate geometric correspondence, area, contour, or pose from segmentation/keypoints.
   - `pitchdisc`: estimate fundamental frequency using signal-processing methods and compare the known frequency relation.
   - `timbredisc`: compare independently defined spectral envelope or temporal descriptors.
   - `motiondir`: estimate displacement vectors or optical flow and verify direction.

   The checker should output both the inferred ground-truth relation and an uncertainty/abstention state. It must operate on the stimulus or physical trace, not on A/B judgments.

   **Causal-independence argument:** The result is computed from known stimulus structure and task semantics, so the adversarial judgment cannot cause the checker’s answer through the model’s perceptual pipeline.

   **Expected information:** Approximately 0.5-0.9 bits if validated accuracy is 90-98%; near 0 bits if the extracted property is only a proxy for the judgment.

   **Failure conditions:** The representation may omit the property humans are intended to judge; thresholds may be arbitrary; the checker can inherit the same preprocessing artifact; or the perturbation metadata may encode intent rather than actual stimulus semantics.

2. **Independent physical sensor basis**

   Reacquire the stimulus or its relevant effect using a sensor modality not used by either judgment system. Examples include a calibrated colorimeter/spectrometer for color, microphone plus calibrated frequency analysis for pitch/timbre, depth camera or motion capture for shape and motion, and force/position sensors for physical transformations.

   The sensor-side decision should be trained or specified from the physical measurement, not from A/B outputs. Calibration samples should be collected before looking at the adversarial judgments.

   **Causal-independence argument:** The sensor has a different transduction path and measurement basis, so a perceptual/model-specific fooling mechanism cannot directly determine its result.

   **Expected information:** Roughly 0.4-0.9 bits at 85-98% validated accuracy; lower if the sensor only indirectly measures the judged property.

   **Failure conditions:** The adversarial perturbation also changes the sensor measurement; the sensor’s bandwidth or calibration is insufficient; the sensor shares a preprocessing algorithm with A/B; or the relevant property is observer-dependent rather than physically measurable.

3. **Human verification with independent presentation and blinding**

   Use human observers who did not construct the stimuli or know the A/B judgments. Present primary, noise, and adversarial variants in randomized, counterbalanced trials, with a forced-choice question tied to the actual task relation. Use multiple observers, record response times and abstentions, and pre-register the aggregation rule. Do not ask humans to predict whether A or B is right; ask them to judge the underlying relation directly.

   **Causal-independence argument:** The human response is generated by an independent observer and presentation path rather than by the model judgment stream; the adversarial judgment cannot be part of the human causal input under blinding.

   **Expected information:** About 0.1-0.5 bits for a difficult perceptual task at 65-85% accuracy; up to 0.7 bits for a clear task near 95% accuracy.

   **Failure conditions:** The adversary fools humans too; observers rely on the same superficial cue; wording or order effects dominate; subjects learn the perturbation pattern; or the stimulus is genuinely ambiguous. Human agreement should therefore be measured, not assumed to equal ground truth.

4. **Physical counterfactual or reversal test**

   Construct a paired intervention that should reverse or remove the suspected change while preserving unrelated properties. For example, undo a geometric transform, restore the original frequency relation, or replay motion with direction reversed. The analytic or sensor-based checker then tests whether the relevant property changes as predicted.

   The key output is not merely “the model changed its answer,” but whether the independently measured consequence of the intervention matches the causal prediction.

   **Causal-independence argument:** The intervention changes the underlying stimulus or physical cause, and the result is assessed through the intervention’s measurable consequence rather than through another model judgment.

   **Expected information:** Around 0.3-0.8 bits when the intervention has a well-defined predicted effect and is measured with 80-95% reliability.

   **Failure conditions:** The intervention changes multiple factors at once; the reversal is not actually the inverse transformation; the adversarial effect is robust to the intervention; or the only observed outcome remains another judgment from the same pipeline.

5. **Causal provenance and generator audit**

   For generated stimuli, independently audit the transformation code, random seed, parameter values, and source stimulus. Recompute the perturbation from a clean implementation and verify the intended semantic relation using formal invariants or exact numerical checks. For example, prove that a `colorconst` transformation preserves the specified color relation, or that `motiondir` changes only the intended vector direction.

   This is most useful when “correct” means correct relative to a known transformation or label-generating process.

   **Causal-independence argument:** The audit operates on the stimulus-generation cause and its declared invariants, before and independently of the judgment systems.

   **Expected information:** Potentially 0.8-1.0 bits when the task has exact digital ground truth and the audit passes; substantially less for perceptual or semantic labels.

   **Failure conditions:** The generator’s declared target is not the same as the task’s semantic target; implementation correctness does not establish perceptual salience; source data are already mislabeled; or the physical rendering/display pipeline alters the intended stimulus.

6. **External task outcome or instrumented consequence**

   Define a downstream observable that should differ depending on whether the adversarial judgment is correct, then measure it with a task-specific instrument. Examples include a robotic actuator responding to detected motion direction, a spectrometer confirming a pitch relation, or a calibrated display/camera loop checking whether a color-constancy relation survives rendering.

   The outcome must be specified before inspecting A/B predictions and must be scored against an independently defined success criterion.

   **Causal-independence argument:** The channel measures a physical or task-level consequence of the disputed property, not the internal judgment that produced the verdict.

   **Expected information:** Roughly 0.2-0.8 bits for an outcome with 80-95% reliability; more if the consequence is deterministic and directly tied to the proposition.

   **Failure conditions:** The downstream task introduces its own confounds; actuator or instrument noise dominates; the task outcome only tests model usefulness rather than correctness; or the same adversarial feature affects both the judgment and the downstream system.

7. **Cross-modality replication with pre-registered agreement rules**

   Re-express the underlying stimulus relation in a genuinely different modality where possible. For example, verify a motion relation using physical position traces, pitch using both waveform frequency and an independent acoustic instrument, or shape using depth geometry rather than RGB appearance. Require agreement between modalities before resolving `SUSPECT`; disagreement should remain `SUSPECT`, not be forced into correct/incorrect.

   **Causal-independence argument:** Independent modalities expose different physical correlates, making a modality-specific adversarial artifact unlikely to determine both results.

   **Expected information:** About 0.3-0.8 bits, depending on modality reliability and residual correlation. If the channels are conditionally independent with accuracies \(p_1\) and \(p_2\), combining them can approach the information of their joint evidence, but not the sum if they share failure modes.

   **Failure conditions:** The modalities are only superficially different; both rely on the same derived features or labels; the perturbation affects the common physical cause; or the underlying property is not modality-invariant.

The install gate should preserve `SUSPECT` whenever these channels disagree, abstain, or have not been validated for the relevant perturbation class. In particular, a second ML model trained on the frozen judgments, confidence values, stimulus IDs, or similar representations would provide apparent resolution but not causal resolution; its measured information could be high on the corpus while remaining close to 0 bits about the real question.
