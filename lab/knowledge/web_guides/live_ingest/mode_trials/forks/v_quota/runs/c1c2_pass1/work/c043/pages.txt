S|# What Is an Earthquake?
S|| NASA Space Place – NASA Science for Kids An **earthquake** is an intense shaking of Earth’s surface.
S|The shaking is caused by movements in Earth’s outermost layer.
S|## Why Do Earthquakes Happen?
S|Although the Earth looks like a pretty solid place from the surface, it’s actually extremely active just below the surface.
S|The Earth is made of four basic layers: a solid crust, a hot, nearly solid mantle, a liquid outer core and a solid inner core.
S|[Image 0: A diagram of the layers of the Earth.] A diagram of Earth's layers.
S|Earthquakes are caused by shifts in the outer layers of Earth—a region called the lithosphere.
S|The solid crust and top, stiff layer of the mantle make up a region called the **lithosphere**.
S|The lithosphere isn’t a continuous piece that wraps around the whole Earth like an eggshell.
S|It’s actually made up of giant puzzle pieces called **tectonic plates**.
S|Tectonic plates are constantly shifting as they drift around on the **viscous**, or slowly flowing, mantle layer below.
S|[Image 1: Map of the Earth's tectonic plates.] Earth's crust is fractured into tectonic plates that have been moving very slowly over the Earth's surface for millions of years.
S|Credit: USGS This non-stop movement causes stress on Earth’s crust.
S|When the stresses get too large, it leads to cracks called **faults**.
S|When tectonic plates move, it also causes movements at the faults.
S|An **earthquake** is the sudden movement of Earth’s crust at a fault line.
S|[Image 2: An aerial photograph of the San Andreas Fault.] This photograph shows the San Andreas Fault, a 750-mile-long fault in California.
S|Credit: Public Domain The location where an earthquake begins is called the **epicenter**.
S|An earthquake’s most intense shaking is often felt near the epicenter.
S|However, the vibrations from an earthquake can still be felt and detected hundreds, or even thousands of miles away from the epicenter.
S|## How Do We Measure Earthquakes?
S|The energy from an earthquake travels through Earth in vibrations called **seismic waves**.
S|Scientists can measure these seismic waves on instruments called seismometer.
S|A seismometer detects seismic waves below the instrument and records them as a series of zig-zags.
S|Scientists can determine the time, location and intensity of an earthquake from the information recorded by a seismometer.
S|This record also provides information about the rocks the seismic waves traveled through.
S|[Image 3: A photograph of a seismometer recording seismic waves as a series of zig-zag lines.] A seismometer records seismic waves as a series of zig-zags.
S|Credit: Wikimedia Commons user Z22, CC BY-SA 3.0 ## Do Earthquakes Only Happen on Earth?
S|Earthquake is a name for seismic activity on Earth, but Earth isn’t the only place with seismic activity.
S|Scientists have measured quakes on Earth's Moon, and see evidence for seismic activity on Mars, Venus and several moons of Jupiter, too!
S|NASA’s InSight mission took a seismometer to Mars to study seismic activity there, known as marsquakes.
S|On Earth, we know that different materials vibrate in different ways.
S|By studying the vibrations from marsquakes, scientists hope to figure out what materials are found on the inside of Mars.
S|[Image 4: An artist's renduring of the Mars InSight lander operating on the surface of Mars] An artist's illustration of the Mars InSight lander operating on the surface of Mars.
S|Credit: NASA-JPL/Caltech InSight is collecting tons of information about what Mars is like under the surface.
S|These new discoveries will help us understand more about how planets like Mars—and our home, Earth—came to be.

S|Categories Earthquakes Table of Contents What’s an Earthquake?
S|An earthquake happens when there’s a lot of stress inside the Earth’s crust (the outer layer of the Earth), and it needs to be released.
S|Now, you might be wondering where this stress comes from.
S|The answer is from the movement of tectonic plates.
S|The Earth’s crust is like a big puzzle made up of large and small pieces, which we call tectonic plates.
S|These plates float on a squishy layer called the mantle and move around very slowly – about the same speed as your fingernails grow!
S|Sometimes, these plates bump into each other and get stuck, causing stress to build up.
S|When this stress is released, it creates waves that we feel as an earthquake.
S|Different Types of Cracks and Their Role in Earthquakes Faults are like cracks in the Earth’s crust where most of the shaking happens.
S|There are three main types of faults: Normal Faults : These happen when the crust stretches and one block of rock slides down compared to another.
S|This type is common in areas where the crust is being pulled apart.
S|Reverse Faults (or thrust faults) : These happen when the crust is squished together, causing one block of rock to be pushed up over another.
S|Reverse faults are common in areas where tectonic plates are colliding.
S|Strike-Slip Faults : These happen when blocks of rock slide past each other sideways.
S|The San Andreas Fault in California is a famous example of a strike-slip fault.
S|Most earthquakes happen along these fault lines.
S|The Pacific Ring of Fire, an area around the Pacific Ocean, has lots of earthquakes because it has many faults.
S|How Do We Measure Earthquakes?
S|Scientists use special machines called seismographs to record earthquakes.
S|These machines measure the waves created by an earthquake.
S|The information they collect is used to figure out how strong the earthquake was and where it started.
S|The strength of an earthquake is called its magnitude.
S|This is measured using the Richter scale, a system created by Charles F.
S|On this scale, each increase by one whole number means the earthquake is ten times stronger and releases about 31.6 times more energy!
S|The epicenter is the spot on the Earth’s surface right above where the earthquake started.
S|Most earthquakes begin less than 50 miles (80 km) below the Earth’s surface, but some can start as deep as 400 miles (640 km).
S|Even though earthquakes can be scary and cause damage, they’re a normal part of how the Earth changes and moves.
S|By learning about earthquakes and the faults that cause them, scientists hope to get better at predicting these events and reducing the damage they cause.

S|# Explaining Tectonic Plates and Earthquakes to Kids / Learning Corner Alright!
S|Let's imagine our Earth as a giant puzzle made up of huge pieces called tectonic plates.
S|These plates are like gigantic jigsaw pieces that fit together on Earth's surface.
S|Sometimes, these plates move around, either away from each other, closer together, or sliding past each other.
S|Now, when these plates move, they can sometimes get stuck because of all the pressure between them.
S|Imagine trying to slide two pieces of cardboard against each other with a lot of force holding them together.
S|When these plates finally move past each other after being stuck for a while, it creates a lot of energy.
S|This energy is released in the form of seismic waves that travel through the Earth and cause the ground to shake, which we feel as an earthquake.
S|For example, think of two friends playing tug-of-war with a rope.
S|If one suddenly lets go, the other friend might fall backward, causing a jolt.
S|Similarly, when tectonic plates finally release their built-up energy, it causes the ground to shake, causing an earthquake.
S|So, tectonic plates moving and interacting with each other are like a giant Earth puzzle always shifting and causing earthquakes when they suddenly release a lot of pent-up energy!
