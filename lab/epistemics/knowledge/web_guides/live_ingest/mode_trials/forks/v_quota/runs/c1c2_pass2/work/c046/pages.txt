S|## British Science WeekHow are weather forecasts created?
S|[Image 0] There is no better time to look deeper into meteorology than on British Science Week.
S|Starting with the world of forecasts.
S|Meteorologists use some of the world’s most powerful super-computers to produce our weather forecasts.
S|These incorporate a vast amount of data and perform trillions of calculations per second to give us an idea of how the weather will change in the coming days.
S|Due to the complexity of the atmosphere however, even these weather forecasts tend to only be reliable for up to five days or so.
S|If the weather conditions are particularly complex, there can still be a degree of error even within the five-day period.
S|Any forecast that offers an outlook greater than ten days’ time is not considered a forecast but a trend.
S|To produce these forecasts, meteorologists usually run their forecast models a number of times and group the forecasts into what are known as clusters.
S|This gives probabilities of what may happen in the future, rather than one single solution.
S|Short-term forecasts cover a period of 24 hours up to three days.
S|We see these on the TV every day.
S|They are generated using synoptic meteorology – current atmospheric observations – and numerical models.
S|Medium-range forecasts cover between three and 10 days with long-term forecasts covering periods over 10 days away.
S|Both are generated by using weather ensemble models to create a weather trend.
S|For both ranges, conditions tend to be described as wetter or drier, warmer or cooler with no specific information.

P|c046-p2|From Past to Future: Understanding the Main Sources of Weather Data - meteoblue
H|www.meteoblue.com
S|**Was last summer unusually dry?
S|Will tomorrow’s winds be strong enough to affect outdoor work?
S|What temperatures should a building be designed to withstand?
S|Each question calls for weather information, but answering them requires different kinds of data.** [Image 0] Weather data gains meaning from how it was produced and the period it describes.
S|A local measurement captures conditions at a particular point, while a model can estimate weather where instruments are absent or calculate how conditions may develop.
S|For agriculture, renewable energy and infrastructure planning, these distinctions have practical consequences.
S|The most suitable source depends on whether the task is to investigate an event, anticipate changing conditions or understand long-term patterns.
S|Four broad categories provide a useful starting point: observations record what instruments measure or detect; forecasts describe possible future weather; reanalyses combine observations and models to reconstruct the past; and climatologies summarise conditions over many years.
S|### **Observations: measuring the atmosphere in real time** Weather observation begins with instruments.
S|Surface stations measure temperature, pressure, precipitation and wind.
S|Weather balloons sample the atmosphere vertically, while ships and buoys extend coverage across the oceans.
S|Radar and satellites provide information over larger areas.
S|These systems observe weather in different ways.
S|A thermometer measures temperature at a particular place and height.
S|Radar uses reflected electromagnetic pulses to detect precipitation and its movement.
S|Satellites measure radiation, from which information about clouds, temperature and moisture can be derived.
S|Together, these sources provide complementary views of the atmosphere.
S|meteoblue uses local measurements alongside radar and satellite observations to build a fuller picture of current weather and assess how well forecasts match actual conditions.
S|Even when these sources are combined, observations have limits.
S|A station records conditions at a specific location, which may differ from those on a nearby mountain slope or coastline.
S|Observing networks are also unevenly distributed, and records may contain gaps or be affected by changes in instruments.
S|Radar and satellites extend coverage, but some weather variables are derived indirectly from the signals they detect.
S|The accuracy of these estimates depends on the instrument and atmospheric conditions.
S|Combining these observations helps fill gaps, although some local weather features may still go undetected.
S|[Image 1] ### **Forecasts: moving the atmosphere forward in time** To determine how the weather may develop, meteorologists rely on numerical models.
S|These models represent the atmosphere through physical equations, calculating changes in air movement, temperature and moisture over the coming hours and days.
S|First, the model needs an estimate of the atmosphere’s current state.
S|A process called data assimilation combines observations with model information to produce an analysis, including estimates for places without direct measurements.
S|The model then advances this starting state forward in time.
S|Forecasts contain unavoidable uncertainty.
S|Small errors in the initial conditions can grow, while models differ in how they represent terrain, clouds and other processes.
S|Confidence generally decreases as the forecast extends further into the future.
S|To account for this uncertainty, ensemble forecasts calculate a range of possible outcomes.
S|Multi-model approaches offer another perspective by comparing results from different forecasting systems.
S|In practice, meteoblue combines numerical model information with measurements and post-processing methods to improve forecasts for individual locations.
S|Forecasts are appropriate for questions about the future: when might rain begin, or how strong could tomorrow’s wind become?
S|They are not records of what eventually happened.
S|Once the forecast period has passed, observations help assess their accuracy.
S|More about this processing is explained in our recent article on what happens after a model run.
S|[Image 2] ### **Reanalysis: rebuilding weather from the past** Suppose we want hourly weather information for a remote location in 1985.
S|There may have been no station there, or its records may be incomplete.
S|Even existing measurements describe individual points rather than the whole atmosphere.
S|Reanalysis reconstructs past weather from historical observations and model calculations, including estimates for places where no measurements were taken.
S|The same modelling system is applied throughout the period, making it easier to compare conditions across different years.
S|ECMWF ERA5 reanalysis, produced for the Copernicus Climate Change Service (C3S), provides hourly information from 1940 onwards.
S|It covers a wide range of atmospheric and surface variables.
S|A reanalysis is not a measurement archive: many values are model-based estimates constrained by available observations.
S|Nor is it simply a collection of old forecasts.
S|It can incorporate observations that arrived after the original forecasts were issued and apply a consistent modelling system across decades.
S|This consistency makes reanalysis valuable for studying long-term variations and supporting agriculture, renewable energy and infrastructure assessments.
S|Nevertheless, its quality depends on the historical observing network and model resolution.
S|Small thunderstorms, local gusts and fine-scale terrain effects may be poorly represented.
S|For example, meteoblue provides reanalysis information alongside other historical simulation datasets, each with strengths and limitations linked to how it was produced.
S|### **Climatology: defining what is typical** Historical weather data describes particular periods or events.
S|Climatology asks what conditions are typical for a location and time of year.
S|A temperature of 30°C may be routine in one place and exceptional in another.
S|Its significance becomes clearer when compared with a long record of local conditions.
S|Climatologies summarise such records statistically.
S|They can describe average temperatures, rainfall, wind distributions or the frequency of frost and hot days.
S|They are calculated from observations or model-based datasets, rather than representing a separate method for estimating weather at an individual moment.
S|The reference period matters.
S|The World Meteorological Organization (WMO) defines climatological standard normals using 30-year periods, with 1991–2020 as the most recent standard period.
S|These are updated every decade.
S|For long-term climate-change assessments, WMO also retains 1961–1990 as a fixed reference.
S|“This month was two degrees warmer than normal” therefore depends on the chosen baseline.
S|In climatology, “normal” has a defined statistical meaning.
S|Historical baselines also provide a reference for climate projections, which estimate how conditions could change in the coming decades.
S|Scientists produce these projections with climate models, running them under different assumptions about future greenhouse-gas emissions.
S|Scenarios with higher or lower emissions allow them to examine several possible futures.
S|A projection might show, for example, how much warmer a region’s summers could become compared with 1991–2020.
S|Other results describe changes in rainfall or the frequency of extreme heat.
S|Comparing these projections with a historical baseline helps explain how future conditions could differ from what we currently consider normal.
S|[Image 3] ### **The same location, four different questions** Consider a vineyard.
S|A sensor reading of 18.4°C at 08:00 is an observation.
S|An expected temperature of 20°C tomorrow afternoon is a forecast.
S|An hourly temperature series reconstructed for summer 1995 may come from reanalysis.
S|The typical September maximum temperature is climatological information.
S|All four use degrees Celsius, but each answers a different question.
S|A reanalysis value should not be treated as proof that a thermometer recorded that temperature at the exact location.
S|Likewise, a climatological average cannot tell us what will happen on a particular afternoon.
S|The source also determines the detail users can expect.
S|A station provides local information; a gridded dataset offers wider coverage while simplifying conditions within each cell; a forecast describes the future with uncertainty.
S|The best choice depends on what you need to know and how much detail the task requires.
S|### **Connected sources, different purposes** In practice, these categories work together.
S|Observations help establish the current atmospheric state and verify forecasts.
S|Historical observations support reanalysis, while long records from observations or simulations provide the basis for climatologies.
S|For those analysing past weather, the meteoblue Dataset API provides access to archived weather datasets, with options to select the variables, time period and geographic area needed for a particular study.
S|Knowing how weather data was produced helps explain why datasets differ and which source best answers a particular question.
S|A station reading, forecast, reanalysis value and climate normal can all be useful – as long as each is interpreted in context.

S|description: #weather #weatherforecasts #ngscience @NGScience https://ngscience.com Weather forecasting is a critical scientific process that involves predicting atmospheric conditions over a specific area at a given time.
S|Utilizing a combination of meteorological data, advanced computational models, and atmospheric research, forecasters can provide predictions that range from immediate short-term weather conditions to long-range forecasts spanning weeks or even months.
S|The accuracy and reliability of these forecasts have profound implications for public safety, economic planning, and daily decision-making for individuals and communities alike.
S|The Evolution of Weather Forecasting The practice of predicting weather has evolved significantly from its ancient roots, where observations of the sky, animals, and nature informed local lore and predictions.
S|The development of meteorology as a science in the 19th and 20th centuries introduced systematic observation and data collection, laying the groundwork for modern forecasting techniques.
S|The advent of satellites, radar technology, and powerful computational models in the latter half of the 20th century revolutionized weather forecasting, making it more accurate and detailed than ever before.
S|The Science Behind Weather Forecasts At the heart of weather forecasting is the collection of data about the current state of the atmosphere, including temperature, humidity, pressure, wind speed and direction, and precipitation.
S|This data is collected from a wide array of sources, including weather stations, balloons, buoys, radar, and satellites.
S|Once gathered, the data is input into sophisticated computer models that simulate the physics of the atmosphere to predict how weather systems will move and evolve.
S|Weather Models and Predictive Tools There are several types of weather models used by meteorologists, ranging from global models that forecast large-scale weather patterns to regional models that provide more detailed predictions for specific areas.
S|These models, including the Global Forecast System (GFS) and the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF), use equations that simulate the fluid dynamics of the atmosphere and the interactions between the atmosphere and the oceans, ice, and land surfaces.
S|The Role of Meteorologists Meteorologists play a crucial role in interpreting model outputs, incorporating their expertise and understanding of local conditions to refine forecasts.
S|They assess model reliability, compare different model predictions, and account for factors that models may not fully capture.
S|This human element is essential, especially for predicting severe weather events such as hurricanes, tornadoes, and blizzards, where timely and accurate forecasts can save lives.
S|Challenges in Weather Forecasting Despite significant advances, weather forecasting remains a challenging science.
S|The chaotic nature of the atmosphere means that small initial differences can lead to vastly different outcomes, a phenomenon known as the "butterfly effect." This inherent uncertainty increases with the length of the forecast period, making long-range forecasts less reliable than short-term predictions.
S|Additionally, predicting specific details, such as the exact timing and location of thunderstorms, remains difficult.
