What causes seasons? The simple answer is that seasons are caused by the angle of our planet. Believe it or not, the Earth is actually titled on its axis. This
means that when the Earth orbits the Sun, some parts of it are closer to the Sun than others and some parts further away!

It takes one year for the Earth to orbit the Sun just one time. When the North Pole is tilted towards the Sun, it's summer in the northern hemisphere countries,
such as the United Kingdom (UK), France and Mexico. Then halfway through the year the North Pole is tilted away from the Sun, this means it's summer in the
southern hemisphere countries, this includes Zambia, Ecuador, and of course Australia.

When it’s summer in the northern hemisphere, it’s winter in the southern hemisphere and vice versa. The closer to the Sun, the warmer it will be on Earth, and
the further away then, the cooler it will be.

And, we don’t forget the seasons in between - spring and autumn.

### How Long Does It Take for the Earth to Orbit the Sun?

It takes one year for the Earth to make a full orbit around the Sun. As we mentioned at the start, because the Earth is tilted on its axis, this is why we
experience four seasons in Australia and many other countries around the world. Think of the axis as an imaginary line which runs through the Earth from the
North to the South Pole. The tilt affects the amount of daylight we get, depending on where we are in the world and how close we are to the Sun.

As the Earth is tilted, the northern and southern hemispheres experience different seasons. If the northern hemisphere is tilted towards the Sun, it will
experience warmer temperatures and summer. Meanwhile, the southern hemisphere is tilted away from the Sun and has colder temperatures - it will therefore be
experiencing winter.

For example, when it’s winter in the northerly UK (December, January, February) it will be summer in southerly Australia!

### Why Do We Have Rainy and Dry Seasons in Australia?

This is all to do with air pressure, according to the Bureau of Meteorology. It says that when the Sun moves closer to the Tropic of Capricorn, Northern
Australia sees the area of low-pressure grow. While this is happening, winter is starting in the northern hemisphere, so here the pressure rises. The air flows
from high to low pressure. Winds warm the closer they get to the equator, and they collect moisture from the tropical oceans - we call this the monsoon, which
typically arrives in Australia in late December. From December through to April, the monsoon can vary across Northern Australia for a number of reasons.

### Why Do We Only Get Two Seasons in Australia’s Tropics?

Australia’s tropics, including the far North of Queensland, the top end of the Northern Territory and Exmouth in Western Australia, only see wet and dry seasons
- but, why is this? Tropical regions are closer to the equator, this means that they see little variation in the amount of heat they receive over the course of
the year, so the temperature doesn’t vary all that much, and a more noticeable difference is the amount of rainfall with a distinct wet and dry season. So,
that’s why we can describe the seasons in the tropics by the amount of rainfall instead of temperature (which relates to the four seasons).

### Why Do We Have Seasons? FAQs:

**When Are The Four Seasons in Australia?**

- Spring - September, October, and November

- Summer - December, January, and February

- Autumn - March, April, and May

- Winter - June, July, and August

**Why Do We Have Winter and Summer Seasons on Earth?**

It’s all to do with the Earth’s tilt, which is known as the axial tilt. According to the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) the axial tilt
varies between ‘22.1 and 24.5 degrees’, during a cycle that averages 40,000 years. So, we can say that the Earth’s axis is tilted at around 23.5 degrees from
the plane of its orbit around the Sun. What this means is that sometimes the top half of the Earth is pointing towards the Sun, and sometimes it is pointing
away. This is why we get seasonal changes, such as winter and summer.

**Does The Sun Rotate Around the Earth?**

The short answer is no! But, let’s explain this in more detail. Firstly, there is a difference between ‘rotation’ and ‘revolution’. ‘Rotation’ is talking about
an object’s spinning motion about its own axis. ‘Revolution’ on the other hand, is the object’s orbital motion around another object. For example, NASA says
that ‘Earth revolves about the Sun, producing the 365-day year’. So, here is the answer to the question: the Sun does not rotate around the Earth, but the Earth
does ‘revolve’ about the Sun. The Earth also ‘rotates’ on its axis, which gives us the 24-hour day. Cool, huh?

**What Causes Day and Night?**

So, we already mentioned how the Earth rotates about its own axis once every 24 hours, and this is why we get day and night on Earth. So, when bits of Earth are
facing the Sun, it is daytime. And, when those bits are facing away from the Earth, it’s nighttime.
