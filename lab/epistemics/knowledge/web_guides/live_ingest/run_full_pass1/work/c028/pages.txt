S|# How do bees make honey?
S|- BBC Science Focus Magazine ## 1.
S|Honey begins as nectar This sugary liquid is produced by flowers to encourage insect pollinators to visit.
S|Nectar is 70-80 per cent water with a mixture of three different sugars: sucrose, glucose and fructose, plus some scent chemicals to help draw bees to the flowers.
S|[Image 0: How do bees make honey?
S|Drinking and storing the nectar Honeybees suck nectar through their hollow proboscis (a straw-like tongue) and store it in the first chamber of their stomach, called the proventriculus.
S|An enzyme called invertase breaks the sucrose down into a simpler glucose and fructose molecules.
S|Other enzymes raise the acidity of the nectar, which helps kill bacteria.
S|[Image 1: How do bees make honey?
S|Travelling back to the hive Bees can carry up to half their body weight and have one of the highest power output rates in the animal kingdom.
S|This lets them forage up to 5km from the hive, but at this range most of the nectar payload is used for the flight home.
S|[Image 2: How do bees make honey?
S|In the hive Back at the hive, forager bees regurgitate the nectar.
S|It’s then passed between worker bees, mouth-to-mouth, to reduce its water levels.
S|Once the water content drops to 18 per cent, mould and bacteria can’t grow and the nectar becomes honey, which the worker bees push into wax chambers.
S|[Image 3: How do bees make honey?
S|Using the honey In spring and early summer, the bee colony uses all its honey to feed the larvae, building its workforce to a summer peak of 50,000 bees.
S|These workers then spend two to three weeks madly gathering nectar to last the winter.
S|It takes 12 bees their entire life to make a teaspoon of honey.

S|So, you know that honey comes from bees, but how do bees make honey?
S|Kids ask this question all the time, and it’s because most grown-ups don’t even know the answer!
S|Here is an easy explanation and a quick-and-tasty recipe to make honey flapjacks to show off your learning.
S|There are many different types of bee, but only the honey bee can make honey.
S|And it all starts when they visit a flower… ### How Bees Make Honey–Step-by-Step The flower that a honey bee visits must contain nectar, otherwise it will move on.
S|Nectar is like sugar water, and this is what bees like–yummers!
S|They suck it up using their tongue which is hollow like a straw–it’s proper name is a proboscis.
S|Now the nectar is inside the bee’s tummy, and its little body then turns the sugar part of nectar (called sucrose) into different kinds of sugar (glucose and fructose).
S|Some of the glucose then gets turned into an acid.
S|By turning some of the nectar into acid, any bacteria is killed so it doesn’t get into the honey.
S|This is why honey can last for years and years.
S|The bee then moves this watery honey mix from its tummy, into its mouth, and then into the honeycombs of the hive.
S|But this new nectar mix is still quite watery, so the clever bees get rid of most of the water by fanning the mix with their wings.
S|The mixture is now thicker and resembles what we know as honey.
S|To protect their honey, the bees will seal the honeycombs with wax.
S|### Those Clever Bees So, how do bees make so much honey?
S|Well, once they find a good patch of flowers that they like, they go back to the hive and do a bee dance to point other bees in the direction of the tasty nectar.
S|In its whole life, a single bee only produces about one and a half teaspoons of honey, but they live in really big groups, which means they can make a lot of honey when they work together.
S|### Different Types of Honey Honey can taste different depending on what flowers the bees have been feasting on.
S|For example, if beekeepers keep their hives close to heather for the bees to enjoy, the honey these bees produce will be much stronger in taste.
S|Different flowers give different flavours.
S|All honey starts off as being clear and runny, however, over time, some honey can set and become thicker and grainy–this is called set honey.
S|It’s easy to get it back to being runny though, just heat for a short time in the microwave, or put the jar into a bowl of warm water.
S|For cooking, it’s often easier if the honey you use is runny.
S|You can use this tip if you choose to make our delicious honey flapjacks… ### Stuff You’ll Need - 200g butter - 200g sugar (we used demerara, but any will do) - 200g honey* - 400g oats - *Large saucepan, wooden spoon, baking tray* ** You can use golden syrup, or a combination of honey and golden syrup depending on what you have in the cupboard.* ### Let’s Get Going Preheat the oven to 180c, 350f, gas 4.
S|Put your butter, sugar, and honey into a large saucepan.
S|Heat it gently until the butter melts and the sugar dissolves.
S|Remove the pan from the heat and stir in the oats.
S|Spoon the mix into a greased baking tray.
S|Flatten it down a little with your spoon–the mix will spread so you don’t have to push it right into the corners or make it really smooth.
S|Get a grown-up to put it into the oven for 15 minutes.
S|It’s ready when it’s golden around the edges but still squidgy in the middle.
S|Leave it to cool and then cut it into squares.

S|# How do honeybees make honey?
S|| HowStuffWorks A **honeybee** starts the honey making process by visiting a flower and gathering some of its **nectar**.
S|Many plants use nectar as a way of encouraging insects (bees, wasps, butterflies, etc.) to stop at the flower.
S|In the process of gathering nectar, the insect transfers pollen grains from one flower to another and pollinates the flower.
S|Most flower nectars are similar to sugar water -- **sucrose mixed with water**.
S|Nectars can contain other beneficial substances as well.
S|To make honey, two things happen: - **Enzymes** that bees produce turn the sucrose (a disaccharide) into glucose and fructose (monosaccharides).
S|See How Food Works for a discussion of food enzymes and saccharides.
S|- Most of the moisture has to be **evaporated**, leaving only about 18-percent water in honey.
S|­Here is a very nice description of the **enzyme** process: An enzyme, invertase, converts most of the sucrose into two six-carbon sugars, glucose and fructose.
S|A small amount of the glucose is attacked by a second enzyme, glucose oxidase, and converted into gluconic acid and hydrogen peroxide.
S|The gluconic acid makes honey an acid medium with a low pH that is inhospitable to bacteria, mold, and fungi, organisms we call microbes, while the hydrogen peroxide gives short-range protection against these same organisms when the honey is ripening or is diluted for larval food.
S|Honey bees also reduce the moisture content of nectar, which gives it a high osmotic pressure and protection against microbes./ This page gives a very nice description of the **evaporation** process: The physical change involves the removal of water, which is accomplished by externally manipulating nectar in the mouth parts and then placing small droplets on the upper side of cells and fanning the wings to increase air movement and carry away excess moisture.
S|The effect is to make honey a very stable food.
S|It naturally resists molds, fungi and other bacteria, allowing it to last for years without refrigeration!
